All experimental measurements presented here have been submitted voluntarily by participants of the MaCFP-4 Pyrolysis Model Calibration Exercise~\cite{MaCFP4Guidelines}. \chapter{Introduction}
In April 2016, it was proposed that the Measurement and Computation of Fire Phenomena (MaCFP) Working Group be expanded to include a subgroup dedicated to the predictive modeling of condensed phase phenomena. The MaCFP Condensed Phase Working Group was thus organized to enable the fire safety science research community to make significant progress towards establishing a common framework for the selection of experiments and the methodologies used to analyze these experiments when developing pyrolysis models. Ultimately, such a framework will support reliable computational predictions of how materials burn, how flames spread, and how fires grow in realistic fire scenarios. Key objectives of this effort include:
\begin{itemize}
 \item Cataloguing current approaches used to parameterize pyrolysis models;
 \item Developing standard data set formats for experimental data on pyrolysis;
 \item Developing requirements for data set quality and establishing a data review committee;
 \item Quantifying the inter-laboratory variability for comparable experimental datasets; and
 \item Assessing the impact of the variability of model parameters on fire growth predictions
\end{itemize}

The experimental and modeling effort of the MaCFP-2 and MaCFP-3 Workshops~\cite{PreliminarySummary, MaCFP3Proceedings} (2021 and 2023) were designed to enable the fire research community to make significant progress towards establishing a common framework for the selection of experiments (and the methodologies used to analyze these experiments) when developing pyrolysis models. For MaCFP-2, a single reference material\footnote{The specific material of interest is a nominally 6~mm (0.236 inch) thick, black, cast PMMA manufactured by Evonik under the tradename: ACRYLITE® cast black 9H01 GT. Note: the identification of any commercial product or trade name does not imply endorsement or recommendation by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, NIST (or any other contributing institution).\\
Today, very limited amounts of the original MaCFP-PMMA remain, and it can no longer be purchased from the original manufacturer and supplier. To allow for expansion of the MaCFP validation dataset, an experimental study~\cite{DeLannoye2025Evaluating} was conducted to quantify differences in fire behavior of different commercially-available PMMA samples and to identify candidates to serve as a replacement for MaCFP-PMMA. In total, 25 unique PMMA samples were screened using mg-scale experiments and 7 were selected for further testing: bench-scale gasification tests and intermediate scale flame spread tests. Two potential replacement candidates, available from distributors in North America and Europe, were identified~\cite{DeLannoye2025Evaluating}.} -- cast black poly(methyl methacrylate), which is referred to here as `MaCFP-PMMA' -- was selected for study because of its tendency to maintain its density while burning, insignificant melt flow, simple decomposition kinetics, and low transparency to infrared radiation. In total, 18 institutions located in 11 different countries submitted experimental measurements (220 unique tests) to the MaCFP-2 Workshop. Modelers from 13 different institutions in 8 countries then analyzed these experimental measurements to develop parameter sets that could be used to describe the thermal decomposition behavior of this PMMA. 

This effort to characterize MaCFP-PMMA was unique from previous experimental \cite{fiola2020comparison,kashiwagi1982study,hirata1985thermal,tewarson1992fire,rhodes1996burning} and computational modeling studies of PMMA flammability \cite{fiola2020comparison,consalvi2008numerical,leventon2015flame,fukumoto2018large}, as it was the first coordinated attempt involving multiple institutions to simultaneously perform a series of pyrolysis experiments across a range of scales, characterize all relevant thermophysical properties of a fully specified material, and to compare the various methodologies for doing so. The coordination of this effort (in particular organizing the MaCFP-2 experimental campaign) has been detailed~\cite{Leventon2022ASTM} and a preliminary summary of experimental measurements presented at MaCFP-2 is available elsewhere~\cite{PreliminarySummary}. 

At MaCFP-3, new measurement data was generated for a pyrolysis model $validation$ exercise~\cite{MaCFP3Proceedings, Leventon2023Gasification}. Participants who submitted pyrolysis models to MaCFP-2 were asked to use their original model parameters\footnote{Material property validation at MaCFP-3: If sufficient agreement between model predictions and experimental measurements was not observed, pyrolysis modelers were allowed to adjust and resubmit parameter sets developed at MaCFP-2 to obtain better agreement. However, new property sets (a) required a description of exactly how and why specific parameters were changed (e.g., incorporation new calibration data or changes in assumed boundary conditions of model calibration) and (b) could not simply be recalibrated  to match the new validation dataset.} to predict (without adjustment to material properties) the results of these validation experiments. A material property set (\href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/blob/master/PMMA/Material_Properties/2021/MaCFP_PMMA_UMD.json}{developed by UMD for the MaCFP-2 Workshop}) was identified that most closely reproduced the selected validation data set. Modelers of coupled condensed/gas phase cases considered at MaCFP-3 and MaCFP-4 (i.e., \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/macfp-db/tree/master/Fire_Growth}{flame spread and fire growth over MaCFP-PMMA}) were encouraged to use this material property set. 

MaCFP-2 and MaCFP-3 measurement data (which can be used as targets for pyrolysis model calibration and validation) and calibrated material property sets have been uniformly formatted and well-documented (i.e., saved with corresponding metadata describing sample preparation, test setup, and experimental conditions) to allow for efficient, automated analysis. All measurements (and related analysis tools) are maintained in a digital, version-controlled, and freely-available online repository: \url{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}~\cite{MaCFP-cond-db}.

\section{Material Selection}
For MaCFP-4, the Condensed Phase Subgroup coordinated an $anaerobic$ pyrolysis model calibration exercise (i.e., material property determination) focused on pine wood. This charring material was selected based on feedback from participants of the MaCFP-3 Workshop~\cite{MaCFP3Proceedings}, a majority of whom suggested (as a critical fire modeling need) studying a new material and/or studying charring/oxidation. This specific wood species was selected because of its widespread use in construction, furnishings, and standard fire testing (e.g., crib fires). 

MaCFP-4 pyrolysis modeling targets include $only$ anaerobic conditions; however, participants are encouraged to develop experimental datasets and corresponding models that will describe oxidation (as will be studied in detail at MaCFP-5). The intent is that this same wood will be studied in future MaCFP modeling exercises (e.g., oxidative pyrolysis, burning, and fire growth studies).

All MaCFP wood samples -- specifically, Eastern White Pine, $Pinus Strobus$ -- were obtained from the same forest/grove in Gardners, Pennsylvania (near Pine Grove Furnace State Park in the USA). This location is indicated by a green symbol in Fig.~\ref{Fig:MaCFP4_Map}. In the spring and summer of 2025 trees were felled and cut in the form of 2.5~cm thick planks (30.5~cm wide by 3.05~m long; nominally 12~in by 1~in. boards, each 10~ft long) and 8.9~cm by 8.9~cm studs (nominally, 4~in. by 4~in.). These two form factors were selected to allow samples to be cut to size for standard cone and gasification experiments in which heating is applied both perpendicular and parallel to the wood grain. Prior to further preparation for specific tests, all wood boards and studs were kiln dried to a core temperature of 70°C for a duration of 72 hours.

Kiln-dried samples were then cut to discs or squares for gram-scale experiments and ground to powder for milligram-scale experiments by organizers of the MaCFP Workshop. This ensured consistency among participating laboratories. Powdered samples were prepared by mechanically reducing wood samples using a hand rasp to generate coarse wood filings. Rasping was performed across the wood grain to produce irregular particles, characteristic of low-energy mechanical comminution, avoiding excessive heating or structural damage associated with high-speed milling. The resulting filings had a characteristic particle size on the order of 1 mm, appropriate for thermogravimetric analysis and selected to ensure reproducible heat and mass transfer conditions while preserving the native heterogeneous structure of the wood\footnote{The method of preparing powdered samples for mg-scale tests was developed by balancing potential issues of testing larger, solid chunks of raw wood (which can have poor thermal contact in the crucible during heating) and testing fine powders produced by cryogenic ball-milling (under some preparation conditions, this may create excessive mechanical degradation of the material). Measurement data recorded in thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) experiments conducted across a range of heating rates and anaerobic conditions examined (for multiple other species of wood) was considered for this analysis~\cite{Bellamy2025Interflam}. In this work, it was found that the coarse filings exhibited smooth, repeatable mass-loss profiles and consistent apparent kinetic parameters, with no evidence of rate distortions indicative of internal heat or mass transfer limitations. The absence of anomalous `shoulders' (in TGA mass loss rate profiles) or delayed devolatilization supports the conclusion that the selected particle size was sufficiently small to ensure near-uniform thermal response, while remaining large enough to preserve the native heterogeneous structure of the wood. This balance was critical for capturing representative solid-phase decomposition behavior under anaerobic conditions with high repeatability between replicate experiments.}. 

Samples were distributed to participating institutions during the summer and fall of 2025 in the form of 100~mm by 100~mm by 25~mm slabs for bench-scale experiments (e.g., cone calorimeter) and 0.5~mg to 5~mg vials of powdered wood for micro-scale experiments (e.g., thermogravimetric analysis, TGA). Samples were prepared and distributed by Dr. Isaac Leventon [National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), USA; \href{mailto:isaac.leventon@nist.gov}{isaac.leventon@nist.gov}] and Dr. Mark McKinnon [UL Fire Safety Research Institute (UL FSRI), USA; \href{mailto:mark.mckinnon@ul.org}{mark.mckinnon@ul.org}]. The majority of sample cutting, packaging, and shipping was performed by Dr. Mark McKinnon (thank you, Mark). For approximately 30~\% of participants, shipping delays, lost packages, and/or customs restrictions delayed shipments or necessitated re-shipments, thus samples may not have been received until late 2025 or early 2026. Readers of this summary report are encouraged to check the \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/releases}{MaCFP Github Repository} for new datasets and revised versions of this manuscript, both of which are regularly updated.

\section{Reporting of Results: The MaCFP Repository}

Experimental measurements were submitted electronically by participating institutions and were organized and made publicly available in the MaCFP Condensed Phase Repository, which is hosted on GitHub \url{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}. Note that the repository is continuously updated, and users are expected to consult the repository regularly for possible additions and/or corrections. This data repository is available to computational groups for fire model calibration and validation. The Condensed Phase Repository is managed by Isaac Leventon and Karen De Lannoye of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). It contains:
\begin{itemize} [noitemsep]
\item A description of selected target experiments, including descriptions of the experimental configuration, measured quantities, and measurement uncertainties (if known);
\item An electronic copy of experimental data organized in simple comma-delimited ASCII files;
\item  An electronic copy of computational results submitted to each of the three workshops, organized in simple comma-delimited ASCII files;
 \item An electronic copy of material property sets (i.e., pyrolysis models) calibrated by the different modeling groups at the main MaCFP Workshops, organized in simple comma delimited ASCII files;
\item Protocols to perform comparisons between experimental data and simulation results based on (provided) post-processing tools.
\item Copies of presentations (lecture slides and/or audio/video recordings) given at each of the three Workshops
\end{itemize}

All measurement data submitted by each institution to the MaCFP-4 Pyrolysis Model Calibration Exercise is organized in a single folder with the institution’s name. A consistent file naming convention is used for all test data (i.e., across all folders). File names indicate the institution name, experimental apparatus, and basic test conditions (e.g., incident heat flux or heating rate; gaseous environment). Measurement data from repeated experiments is saved in separate files, each numbered sequentially.

Also included in each folder is a README.md file that provides a description of the test conditions of all experiments conducted and submitted. Participants were asked to provide a written description of test setup and procedure and to clearly define the conditions associated with the experiments conducted, as noted in Table~\ref{Table:Tests_Performed}.

\chapter{Experiments Conducted}
MaCFP-4 pyrolysis modeling targets included $only$ anaerobic conditions; however, participants were encouraged to develop experimental datasets and corresponding models that will describe oxidation (as will be studied in detail at MaCFP-5). The intent is that this same wood will be studied in future MaCFP modeling exercises (e.g., oxidative pyrolysis, burning, and fire growth studies).

No single approach for pyrolysis model parameterization was suggested by the organizing committee of the MaCFP Condensed Phase Subgroup. In fact, a key objective of this workshop is to catalog the current approaches used to parameterize pyrolysis models. Participating institutions were therefore encouraged to follow their own best practices when selecting and conducting experiments so that discussions could be organized at the MaCFP-4 workshop regarding the similarities and differences of their approaches and their results (i.e., final parameterization) without explicitly requiring a final validation versus a given test. 

Some participants were able to submit comprehensive measurement datasets (e.g., bench-scale burning or gasification experiments at multiple heat fluxes or mg-scale and bench-scale experiments that allow for unique calibration of individual material properties).  Other groups submitted more targeted datasets that may or may not allow for complete calibration of all material properties of interest. Members of the MaCFP Organizing Committee welcome and appreciate all contributions from each of our participants. Modelers who wish to use these datasets for material property calibration are encouraged to read the associated README.md files for more information.

It was expected that participating institutions would submit data from experiments such as thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), the Cone Calorimeter, gasification experiments (e.g., in a controlled atmosphere cone calorimeter~\cite{babrauskas1992cone} or controlled atmosphere pyrolysis apparatus~\cite{swann2017controlled}), and the Fire Propagation Apparatus (FPA). Although participants could conduct any experiments that they deemed necessary to  provide calibration data for pyrolysis model development, a key objective of this effort was to quantify the inter-laboratory variability for comparable experimental datasets. Thus, recommended test conditions (e.g., incident heat flux in cone calorimeter tests or heating rate in TGA tests) were defined for standard experiments so that, if participants conducted any of those tests as part of their pyrolysis model development, they could do so under similar conditions to allow for direct comparison of results from different laboratories. Further details were provided in the MaCFP-4 Guidelines for Participation~\cite{MaCFP4Guidelines}, which was first shared March 21, 2025.

To date, \textcolor{red}{experiments have been performed in **11 unique apparatus under a combination of **45 different sets of test conditions} (e.g., incident heat flux, heating rate, and/or gaseous environment). A brief description of each of the test apparatus used by participating institutions is provided in Sections~\ref{mg_tests} and \ref{g_tests} of this document. More information regarding these techniques is available in the literature \cite{brown2001introduction,SFPEHandbookThermalDecomp}. Detailed descriptions of the specific test conditions used by participating labs are available on the \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/tree/master/Wood/Calibration_Data}{MaCFP repository}.

\section{Milligram-Scale Tests}
\label{mg_tests}

\subsection{Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)}
In TGA experiments, small samples (typically 3~mg to 10~mg) are heated at a constant rate (2~K/min$ \le\beta\le50$~K/min) or maintained at constant elevated temperature in a carefully controlled gaseous environment (either inert or oxidative). Time-resolved measurements of sample mass and temperature are recorded. Care should be taken to ensure that crucibles and thermocouples selected for use in TGA tests are both compatible with the sample and test conditions of interest and that the instrument is calibrated for use under those specific test conditions (i.e., atmosphere and heating rate). Particular attention should be paid to temperature calibration, which should cover the full temperature range of the experiments for each heating rate. Blank or baseline measurements can also be used to correct for apparent changes in sample mass associated with buoyancy and instrumental drift during the heating program. Further detail regarding the principles and practices of TGA is available elsewhere \cite{coats1963thermogravimetric}.

The combination of small sample size and relatively low heating rate used in TGA experiments allows for the assumption of infinitely fast transport processes, decoupling thermal degradation reactions from heat and mass transfer effects. Analysis of TGA mass and mass loss rate data thus allows for the study of condensed-phase thermal decomposition reaction mechanisms and the determination of the kinetics of these reactions.

Seventeen unique institutions submitted TGA measurements conducted in inert and oxidative gaseous environments. Tests in inert conditions were performed in nitrogen, while oxidative experiments were conducted in oxygen-nitrogen mixtures containing 3, 6, 10, 20, or 21 \% oxygen. Measurements were performed at nine different heating rates ranging from 2~K/min to 50~K/min and, for selected experiments, at two different pressures. One institution also provided TGA data from isothermal tests conducted at 593~K. For each of these experiments, powdered samples between 1~mg and 15~mg were loaded into crucibles, weighed, and then heated from ambient temperature through their primary decomposition reactions at a constant rate. Prior to heating, some samples were held isothermally for approximately 2~min to 30~min to allow the sample and instrument to approach thermal equilibrium and the balance signal to stabilise. In some experiments, this conditioning step was performed at elevated temperature, which may have affected the initial sample moisture content. After heating, some samples were kept under isothermal conditions for approximately 1~min to 30~min.

More information, including apparatus specifications, calibration details, and sample preparation, conditioning, and testing procedures, is provided in the README files for each experiment. This documentation is available on the MaCFP repository \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}. Tables~\ref{Table:Matrix_TGA_N2} and \ref{Table:Matrix_TGA_O2} show the number of TGA tests conducted under each combination of test conditions in nitrogen and oxygen-nitrogen atmospheres, respectively. By far the largest interlaboratory dataset was obtained for a heating rate of 10~K/min in nitrogen, for which measurements from 15 institutions are available. Thirteen of these institutions provided at least three replicate measurements, making this condition particularly suitable for assessing interlaboratory variability.

\subsection{Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)}
In DSC experiments, a small material sample is placed in a crucible of high thermal conductivity and heated at a constant rate alongside a reference crucible in a carefully controlled gaseous environment. Unlike TGA, sample mass is not continuously measured during the experiment; rather, the initial sample mass is determined prior to the test, while time-resolved measurements of sample temperature and heat flow are recorded. Heat flow to the sample can be measured in two ways: (1) power compensation DSC, in which the energy (supplied by electric current) needed to maintain the sample crucible at the same temperature as the reference crucible is directly measured, or (2) heat flux DSC, in which the temperature difference between the sample and reference pans is carefully measured during heating and prior calibration of the instrument allows for the determination of heat flux as a function of this temperature difference. Further detail regarding the principles and practices of differential scanning calorimetry is available elsewhere \cite{mcnaughton2003differential}.

DSC is primarily used to identify and characterize endothermic and exothermic thermal events, including the temperatures at which they occur and the associated heats of reaction or transition. Under appropriately controlled and calibrated conditions, DSC can also be used to determine the heat capacity of a material. These features of interest can be determined based on the deviations of measured heat flow to the sample versus from the baseline (heat flow to empty crucibles). In DSC tests, the baseline is not necessarily easy to establish. The baseline itself may deviate from zero for a variety of reasons including a mismatch of the thermal properties of the sample and the reference material, poor positioning of crucibles themselves or of the samples within their crucible, and/or asymmetry in the construction of sample and reference holders. Excellent thermal contact between the sample, the reference pans, and the instrument and careful calibration of the instrument for the exact test conditions used in each experiment is essential to obtaining accurate measurements of sample heat capacity or heats of decomposition. In addition to temperature calibration (similar to TGA), DSC also requires calibration of the heat-flow sensitivity, which relates the measured instrument response to the actual heat flow. This sensitivity calibration can be particularly challenging at high heating rates. Both temperature and sensitivity calibrations should cover the relevant temperature ranges and heating rates used in the experiments.

Seven institutions submitted DSC measurements. Six institutions provided measurements conducted in pure nitrogen, while one institution also provided measurements in oxygen-nitrogen atmospheres containing 3, 6, 10, or 21 \% oxygen. All submitted DSC measurements were obtained in Simultaneous Thermal Analysis (STA) mode described below, meaning that sample mass was recorded simultaneously with heat flow. Thus, in addition to the DSC signal, corresponding thermogravimetric data are available for these experiments, which is not inherent to conventional DSC measurements. Across all experiments, measurements were conducted at heating rates between 2.5~K/min and 50~K/min. For most of these experiments, powdered samples between 3~mg and 11~mg were loaded into metallic crucibles, weighed, and then heated from ambient temperature through complete thermal decomposition at a constant heating rate.

More information, including  apparatus specifications, calibration details, and sample preparation, conditioning, and testing procedure, is provided in the README files for each experiment. This documentation is available in the MaCFP repository \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}. \subsubsection*{Temperature-Modulated Differential Scanning Calorimetry TM-DSC}
In TM-DSC experiments, a small material sample is placed in a crucible of high thermal conductivity and heated alongside a reference crucible in a carefully controlled gaseous environment. Unlike in traditional DSC tests, in TM-DSC, heating rate is modulated by applying a cyclic heating rate on top of the constant rate. Thus, the time temperature profile in these tests may take the form:
\begin{equation}
T(t)=T_0+\beta*t + C_0*sin(\omega t)
\end{equation}
Here, $\beta$ defines the constant heating rate and $C_0$ and $\omega$ define the amplitude and period of the temperature modulation. 

A key advantage of TM-DSC is its ability to deconvolute reversable (e.g., sensible enthalpy rise or melting events) and non-reversable thermal events (e.g., crystalization or decomposition reactions).  TM-DSC can also help to distinguish TM-DSC overlapping thermal events. Further detail regarding the principles and practices of temperature-modulated differential scanning calorimetry is available elsewhere \cite{mcnaughton2003differential, leng2013materials}.

One institution, ‘Mallard’, provided TM-DSC data in which samples were heated and cooled (three times in each test) from 267~K to 467~K. Tests were repeated three times (3x) using a heating rates of $\beta=3$~K/min. In this report (and on the Github repository), `Reversing' and `Non-Reversing' heat flow signals are identified as such; however, total heat flow measured during these TM-DSC tests is simply identified as `Heat Flow (W/g)', consistent with traditional DSC measurements.

\subsubsection*{Simultaneous Thermal Analysis (STA)}
In STA experiments, a small material sample is placed in a crucible of high thermal conductivity and heated at a constant rate alongside a reference crucible in a carefully controlled gaseous environment. In these tests, both sample mass (TGA) heat flow to the sample (DSC) are recorded during a single, combined experiment. In this report, the thermogravimetric signal obtained from STA experiments is combined with data from conventional TGA measurements conducted under corresponding test conditions. The heat-flow signal is treated as DSC data; in the present dataset, all submitted DSC measurements were obtained using STA instruments.

\subsection{Microscale Combustion Calorimetry (MCC)}
In MCC experiments, mg-scale samples are heated in a carefully controlled (typically anaerobic) gaseous environment, similar to TGA tests. Gaseous pyrolyzates produced by the sample are transported in an inert gas stream to a high-temperature furnace where they are mixed with excess oxygen and allowed to burn to completion. Measurement of total gas flow and oxygen concentration downstream of the furnace allows for the calculation of sample heat release rate due to this non-flaming combustion, based on the principle of oxygen consumption calorimetry. The heat of complete combustion is obtained from the time integral of this heat release rate and measurements of initial and final sample mass. Further detail regarding the principles and practices of MCC is available elsewhere \cite{lyon2013principles}.

Six institutions provided measurement data from approximately 60 MCC test repetitions conducted in five different gaseous environments at three different heating rates. Additionally, two institutions conducted MCC experiments on char (formed in MCC tests under nitrogen atmosphere) in air atmosphere. In each test, powdered samples between 1~mg and 12~mg were loaded into ceramic crucibles, weighed, and then introduced into the MCC pyrolyzer. Often, samples/crucibles are held in the pyrolyzer for 10~min at approximately 350~K (to allow for equilibrium conditions: system temperature and gaseous environment) and then heated from this temperature through complete thermal decomposition at a constant heating rate. Additional information, including  apparatus specifications, calibration details, and sample preparation, conditioning, and testing procedure, is provided in the README files for each experiment, which can be found in the MaCFP repository \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}. \subsection{Cone Calorimeter}
\label{g_tests_cone}
In cone calorimetry experiments, gram-scale samples (typically 10~cm square) are placed on a layer of thermal insulation inside of a steel holder and exposed to a conical, radiant heater capable of providing an external heat flux of 10~kW/m$^2$ to 75~kW/m$^2$. Samples can be supported in either the horizontal or vertical configuration (typically horizontal) with or without a spark igniter.  This provides (nominally) a one-dimensional heating scenario where sample slabs can burn freely in well-ventilated, open-atmosphere conditions. Samples are supported above a load cell and positioned beneath a well-instrumented exhaust hood, capable of providing oxygen consumption measurements. Thus, the apparatus produces time-resolved measurements of sample mass loss and heat release rates. Additional test conditions, such as specimen thickness and conditioning, type and thickness of backing insulation, wrapping, or metal frame, may vary between experimental setups and can significantly affect the measured response. These conditions should therefore be clearly documented when reporting cone calorimetry results. Further detail regarding the operating principles, best practices, complete capabilities, and analysis techniques of cone calorimetry is available elsewhere \cite{babrauskas1984development, SFPEHandbookCone, astm1354standard, twilley1988user}.

Cone calorimeter test results can be used to determine a material's heat of combustion ($\Delta H_{\rm{c}}$ [kJ/g]) and representative burning behavior of a cross-section of a material or product in response to known heating conditions. In this pyrolysis model calibration exercise, some experimentalists were able to repeat cone calorimeter experiments in which the wood grain orientation was aligned parallel or perpendicular to the sample's top surface (different sample types were provided, cut from different cross sections of boards otherwise obtained from the same trees). Multiple labs also repeated experiments including measurements of back, front, and/or in-depth temperature temperature rise of wood samples during heating.

Ten institutions submitted experimental measurements from 91 unique cone calorimeter tests conducted using an applied external heat flux of 25, 30, 45, 50, 60, and 75~kW/m$^2$. For all of these tests, samples were 25~mm thick and 10~cm square. Tests were conducted both with and without frames (to keep samples in place within their holders) using a variety of backing insulation layers. Both of these features may impact the burning behavior of samples during experiments. Additional information, including apparatus specifications and calibration details, backing insulation thermal conductivity, and sample preparation, conditioning, and testing procedure, is provided in the README files for each experiment, which can be found in the MaCFP repository \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}. \textbf{Controlled Atmosphere Cone Calorimetry}\\
In controlled atmosphere cone calorimetry, a standard cone calorimeter (described above) is modified by building an enclosure around the heater and the sample platform. By supplying a well-defined mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, and/or air to this enclosure, a reduced oxygen environment can be maintained around the sample. Further information regarding the development of controlled atmosphere cone calorimetry and an explanation of how the controlled‐atmospheres unit is operated is provided elsewhere \cite{babrauskas1992cone}.

\textbf{Controlled Atmosphere Pyrolysis Apparatus (CAPA)}\\
The most recent version of this apparatus, CAPA~II, was used to obtain the reported data. CAPA~II provides a well-defined, axi-symmetric, one-dimensional heating environment in which a disc-shaped sample (7~cm diameter) is exposed to radiant heat supplied by the heating element of a cone calorimeter. The oxygen concentration around the sample can be reduced below 1~\% by supplying a co-flow of pure nitrogen around the sample. The sample rests on a painted copper foil exposed to the atmosphere to enable a non-intrusive back surface temperature measurement. In each experiment, time-resolved measurements of sample mass, back surface temperature, and sample profile evolution are recorded simultaneously. Additional information about CAPA~II, including quantification of boundary conditions, measurement capabilities and technique, and sample preparation, conditioning, and testing procedure, is available elsewhere \cite{swann2017controlled}.

\subsection{Thermal Conductivity and Diffusivity}
\label{g_tests_conductivity}
“Direct measurements” of thermal conductivity were provided by two institutions, while thermal diffusivity measurements were provided by three institutions. Two measurement techniques were used: the Transient Plane Source (TPS) Hot Disk method \cite{gustafsson1991transient} and the Laser Flash method \cite{NeztschLFA}. Experiments were conducted on virgin wood samples either at room temperature or over a temperature range from 298 K (25 °C) to 573 K (300 °C). Thermal diffusivity measurements were performed for samples with the grain oriented both parallel and perpendicular to the sample surface, allowing the effect of grain orientation on heat transfer through the material to be assessed. A pronounced influence of grain orientation was observed. \subsection{Compositional Analysis}
\label{g_tests_composition}
\subsubsection{Elemental Composition (CHNS-O)}
Elemental compositions were determined using a Thermo Fisher Flash 2000 organic elemental analyzer, which features two analytical channels for measuring either (1) carbon, nitrogen, hydrogen, and sulfur by combustion, or (2) oxygen by pyrolysis. Ultra-high purity (UHP – 99.999\%) helium (He) flows through the system, acting as a carrier gas for combustion and pyrolysis products. In the combustion reactor, powdered samples (1.5~mg to 3.5~mg) are placed in tin capsules and introduced into the 950~°C reactor, where a pulse of UHP oxygen facilitates combustion. Within the reactor, combustion products are converted into a mixture of known gasses (N$_{2}$, CO$_{2}$, H$_{2}$O, and SO$_{2}$) based on sample composition. Quantification of these species is achieved through gas chromatography thermal conductivity detection (GC-TCD). Products are temporally separated in a GC column and transported to the TCD. The voltage signal generated by this detector is quantified vs time as a chromatogram.  Peaks in the chromatograms represent each analyte, with the peak areas being proportional to individual species (and therefore elemental) mass. The pyrolysis reactor follows the same operational principle, although samples are placed in silver capsules and introduced into the 1060~°C reactor without oxygen. The material fully pyrolyzes within the reactor, and oxygen content is quantified by the CO peak within the chromatogram.

Calibration curves converting chromatogram peak area to element mass were developed using the set of analytical standards tjat have a well-defined composition. Analysis of each standard was repeated at low (1.5~mg $\pm$ 0.1~mg) and high (3.5~mg $\pm$ 0.1~mg) mass. In combination with the disparate compositions in the standard set, this created atomic mass bounds that would include most materials analyzed in the 1.5 to 3.5~mg range. Validation tests using the same analytical standards as unknowns (at 2.5~mg $\pm$ 0.1~mg) demonstrated that, on average, mass fractions of each element are measured within 2~\% of their reference values~\cite{tripi2025measurement}. 

\subsubsection{Molecular Weight of Gaseous pyrolyzates}
An experimental method was developed to identify the average molecular formula of the gaseous volatiles produced during anaerobic decomposition of combustible solids; this approach was applied to MaCFP-Wood. In this approach, an organic elemental analyzer determines the average composition, while a heated, non-stirred pressure vessel determines the average molecular weight. In the pressure vessel experiments, gram-scale samples are heated under inert atmospheres (helium) to measure the average molecular weight of the evolved gaseous species via ideal gas law calculations. Calibration testing was performed without any sample to ensure the assumption of ideality via proportional pressure and temperature rise. Validation tests were conducted using water or naphthalene, which are both known to boil or sublimate, without chemical decomposition, to create gaseous species of known composition and molecular weight. Measured molecular weights matched within 4.7~\% of expected values. The full experimental approach has been applied to two synthetic polymers known to degrade predominantly into their monomers and to a natural wood, western red cedar. Further details of this approach are available elsewhere~\cite{tripi2025measurement}.

\subsection{Sample Reflectivity and Emissivity}
\label{optical_tests}
Measurements of spectrally-dependent sample reflectivity were determined using experimental apparatuses consisting of an FTIR spectrometer coupled with a detector and an integrating sphere. First, a reference measurement is performed in the sphere without a sample (system sealed with the same gold coating), then the flux reflected by the sample is measured, and finally the sample reflectivity is calculated as the ratio between reflected flux and reference flux. Various combinations of sources, detectors, and integrating spheres can be used for sample reflectivity measurements. Here, three institutions provided reflectivity data for virgin wood samples, and two institutions also provided data for charred wood samples. The char samples were obtained from specimens previously exposed in CAPA or cone calorimeter experiments at known incident heat fluxes. Additional information, including experimental set-up and calibration details, is provided in the README file of each experiment.  In these tests, directional-hemispherical reflectivity ($\rho$) is measured at wavelengths between 1.4~$\mu$m and 10~$\mu$m. As wood samples are opaque, their transmissivities are assumed equal to 0 and their absortivities are calculated as the complement of reflectivity to 1: $\alpha=1-\rho$. More information about this experimental technique is available elsewhere~\cite{boulet2012characterization}.


\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{exp_results}
In this section, experimental measurements from both mg- and g-scale tests are presented. When tests were conducted under the same nominal experimental conditions (e.g., heating rate, incident heat flux, and/or gaseous environment) and when data provided by different institutions showed qualitative agreement, average curves (e.g., heat release rate or mass loss rate vs. time or temperature) were calculated; these mean values are identified throughout the text by symbols with overbars. Type A uncertainties~\cite{taylor1994nist} of these mean values are reported for all data as two standard errors of the mean calculated over at least three independent observations, unless otherwise stated.

It should be noted that some variations between datasets are simply stochastic (i.e., random, unavoidable noise in repeated tests); however, others may result from systematic causes. For mg-scale experiments, these may include differences in temperature and sensitivity calibration, actual heating rate, crucible configuration, purge-gas conditions, sample mass, or initial moisture content. For g-scale experiments, systematic differences may arise from incident heat-flux calibration, spatial non-uniformity of the imposed heat flux, specimen density, moisture content, backing insulation, sample holder or frame configuration, grain orientation, and ignition procedure. Consequently, although clear outliers are not considered when calculating average curves (see further discussion throughout this section on how outliers are identified), care should be taken to understand if/how underlying differences in test conditions or procedure may have impacted the response of samples during experiments and thus how this may affect the final averaged dataset. Ultimately, average curves represent the aggregate of data as received, some of which may require corrections by the original submitting institution (e.g., if a dataset was incorrectly labeled or submitted).

The measurement data presented here reflect the status of the MaCFP database as **of September 16, 2026. The analysis builds on the preliminary results presented in June 2026 in La Rochelle, France, where several approaches to interlaboratory comparison were introduced, including assessment of repeatability and reproducibility and evaluation of systematic differences between datasets. Since then, the database has been expanded with additional measurements and contributions from several institutions, and the analyses have been updated accordingly. The data treatment presented in this report reflects the current outcome of the discussions within the MaCFP community and the analyses considered most useful for interpretation of the available dataset. While this report represents the final summary of the present MaCFP-4 experimental analysis, it should not be regarded as defining the full extent of analyses that can be performed using the database. The underlying data may support further or alternative analyses in the future where these are scientifically justified or respond to specific needs of the community. The measurement data remain available in \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/tree/master/Wood/Calibration_Data}{the MaCFP repository}.

As can be seen in Fig.~\ref{Fig:TGA_5K_dTdt}, some of the heating rate data follows a step like function, e.g 5~K/min submitted by 'Cayuga'. This behavior arises from the low temporal resolution of the submitted data, this effect becomes bigger for higher heating rates. When the data are interpolated to 0.5~K temperature intervals (prior to submission), the time resolution should be chosen to preserve the original resolution of the heating-rate data, $\beta$. Specifically, the required time resolution is the original temperature resolution divided by the heating rate.

As shown in Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA_dTdt}, the actual heating rate can vary significantly during the early stages of testing, typically within approximately the first 50--150~K of temperature increase, before approaching its nominal value. In most cases, this behaviour reflects the dynamic response of the instrument furnace and its temperature-control system and is therefore strongly instrument dependent. Such deviations are difficult to eliminate completely, as they depend on factors such as furnace design, controller settings, and the prescribed temperature program. The effect generally becomes more pronounced at higher nominal heating rates, where the furnace and control system must respond more rapidly to maintain the programmed temperature increase. For a small number of tests, more pronounced departures from the nominal heating program, including temporary overshoots, undershoots, or discontinuities, were observed. Some of these features arise from intentionally programmed steps in the experimental procedure; for example, the discontinuity observed for `Orpington' at 10~K/min results from rapid heating to 373~K followed by an extended isothermal hold for sample drying before the subsequent constant-rate heating stage.

Typically, the heating rate stabilised before substantial sample decomposition occurred. In most cases, the stabilised value was close to the nominal heating rate; however, for some experiments, a persistent offset remained and the prescribed heating rate was not fully reached even after the initial transient period. Deviations from the prescribed heating program may be particularly important in DSC and STA measurements, where both transient and persistent heating-rate errors can influence the measured heat-flow signal and consequently quantities derived from it, such as heat capacity or heats of reaction. For experiments conducted by a single institution at the same nominal heating rate but under different gaseous environments or pressures, no consistent systematic differences in the time-resolved heating-rate profiles were apparent; rather, the profiles were generally highly repeatable within each institution.

The practical importance of deviations between nominal and actual heating rate depends on the process or property being studied. They may be particularly important for low-temperature phenomena and for quantities directly linked to the temperature program, such as heat capacity in DSC or STA experiments. If TGA, DSC, or STA data are subsequently used for calibration or parameter optimization, assuming a constant heating rate where the actual rate varies may introduce systematic errors. The actual heating rate profile should therefore be checked over the temperature interval of interest and, where necessary, the measured time-dependent heating rate should be used instead of the nominal constant value. This is illustrated further in Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA_dTdt_ranges}, where the approximate temperature ranges associated with different physical and chemical processes are highlighted. At $\beta$~=~5~K/min, the largest deviations are concentrated mainly within the evaporation range, whereas the heating rate is relatively stable during pyrolysis. At $\beta$~=~30~K/min, deviations persist over a broader temperature range; the `Rouen' and 'Alabio' data also suggest that a strong endothermic reaction slightly affects the measured heating rate.

\subsubsection{Mass Fraction and Mass Loss Rate}

\textbf{Data Processing}\\
 The key measurements recorded during TGA tests are sample mass, $m$~(mg); temperature, $T$~(K); and the time, $t$~(s), at which these two values are recorded. Both $m$ and $T$ were reported as time-resolved measurements. Prior to further analysis, sample mass was normalized by initial mass, $m_0$, which was calculated as the mean value over the first 5 data points of the respective experiment data file.

Most TGA tests were run at nominally constant heating rates between 2~K/min and 50~K/min; approximately 80~\% of the dynamic tests were conducted at heating rates between 5~K/min and 20~K/min. The largest dataset was obtained at 10~K/min, comprising 79 experiments, under different atmospheres, from 15 institutions. One institution also conducted tests under isothermal conditions at 593~K. Sample mass and temperature were reported either at regular temperature increments or at fixed time intervals. The most common reporting interval was 0.5~K; less frequently, data were reported at 1~K temperature increments or at fixed time intervals of approximately 1~s to 2.4~s. Reporting frequency was not necessarily constant and, due to the nature of the experiment, measured sample temperature could actually decrease from one time step to the next. For all tests, time, $t$, and normalized mass, $m/m_0$, signals were first processed using linear interpolation such that they were each reported at the same regular intervals i.e., every 0.5~K. 

Normalized mass loss rate was calculated for each replicate $j$ at each temperature, $T_i$, using a central numerical derivative across a 1~K temperature interval, as follows
\begin{equation}
   \frac{\dot{m}_{i,j}}{m_{0,j}} = \frac{1}{m_{0,j}}\frac{\left(m_{i-1,j}-m_{i+1,j}\right)}{\left(t_{i+1}-t_{i-1}\right)}.
\end{equation}
In this way, the time step, $\Delta t$, used for this calculation could vary (see previous discussion on heating rates), but it would always correspond to a 1~K temperature interval. Noise in the resulting normalized mass loss rate curves was subsequently reduced by applying a Savitzky-Golay filter (third-order polynomial fit; 41-point window). **The same filter was also applied to the normalized mass curves for their subsequent presentation and analysis. These filtering parameters were selected by trial and error and subsequently verified using representative TGA data to ensure that random noise was reduced without significantly affecting the relevant features of the mass-loss-rate curves.
%**COMMENT: filering was not applied to the majority of te normalized mass curves only to the curve that compares different pressures. 

Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA_N2_10K_2} shows the effect of the selected filter on a representative TGA dataset (data submitted by ‘Alabio’ from a test in pure nitrogen at 10~K/min). Filtered and unfiltered normalized mass data are shown on the left axis (green and blue curves, respectively), together with filtered and unfiltered normalized mass loss rate on the right axis (black and red curves, respectively). The selected filter effectively reduces random noise while preserving the relevant features of the signal: individual mass loss events remain clearly resolved, no appreciable shift in their onset or peak temperatures is introduced, and the peak mass loss rate is well preserved.

TGA measurements were analyzed to calculate average mass and MLR curves as well as an estimate of measurement uncertainty for each of these conditions. An effort was made to include as much data as possible in the generation of these average curves; however, clear outliers (full curves, not just individual data points) were not used for the calculation of mean and standard deviation values. Datasets identified as outliers\footnote{Data were excluded from the calculation of average curves for the following reasons: (1) all `Muscovy' data were excluded because measurements were performed using a custom thermogravimetric setup and the actual heating-rate profile differed substantially from the submitted nominal heating rate of 20~K/min; (2) all `Cayuga' data were excluded because the decomposition peak temperature was consistently higher than in the other submissions and the datasets were therefore considered outliers; (3) `Orpington' data below 400~K were excluded because of a sharp initial decrease in mass associated with an isothermal step; and (4) `Pomeranian' data below 380~K were excluded to avoid an initial abrupt decrease in mass loss rate, which produced unrealistically high values of $\dot{m}/m_0$ during the first seconds of the test, particularly at 5~K/min.} are still plotted in Figs.~\ref{Fig:TGA_N2_5K} through \ref{Fig:TGA_O2-21_10K}.% with a note identifying them as outliers in the captions below their respective figures. 

For brevity, this document supplies figures of TGA measurement data only when repeated measurements under those test conditions were supplied by multiple labs; this represents the majority of TGA data received. Normalized mass and mass loss rate curves (individual test data, along with average curves) from all TGA experiments conducted by all labs and under all experimental conditions (all heating rates and gaseous environments) are supplied in Appendix~\ref{App:TGA}.

When repeated measurements were available (submitted by the same or different lab(s) and conducted under the same nominal heating rate and gaseous environment) average mass loss rate was calculated at each temperature, $T_i$, as the mean value reported from all (excluding noted outliers) repeated tests, $j$:
\begin{equation}
\overline{\left(\frac{\dot{m}}{m_0}\right)_i} = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_i} \sum_{j=1}^{N_j} \sum_{i'=i-n}^{i+n} \frac{\dot{m}_{i',j}}{m_{0,j}}
\end{equation}
where $N_i$ is equal to the number of measurements from an individual experiment, $j$, reported in the interval $(i-n) \leq i' \leq (i+n)$. Since the data were interpolated at 0.5~K intervals, $n=2$ corresponds to a temperature interval of $\pm1$~K around $T_i$. $N_j$ is the number of replicate tests. The standard error of the mean, $\sigma_{mean,i}$, was calculated at each temperature, $T_i$, as:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{mean,i} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_i\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_i-1\right)} \sum_{j=1}^{N_j} \sum_{i'=i-n}^{i+n} \left(\frac{\dot{m}_{i',j}}{m_{0,j}} - \overline{\left(\frac{\dot{m}}{m_0}\right)_i}\right)^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Accounting for Initial Sample Moisture Content (Dry-Mass Normalization)}\\
Due to variations in sample preparation and/or testing conditions, at low temperatures initial mass loss varied  between measurements obtained by different laboratories. Apparent moisture content was therefore calculated as $1-m_{400}/m_0$, where $m_{400}$ is equal to the measured sample mass at $T$~=~400~K. This value was then used to re-normalize sample mass, as seen in Fig.~\ref{Fig:TGA_N2_10K_drynorm}. Apparent moisture content (MC) from each dataset is reported in Table~\ref{tab:wood_tga_results}, ranging between 0~$\%$ and 8.2~$\%$. These differences do not necessarily reflect variations in the original material itself, but are strongly affected by sample conditioning and the experimental procedure prior to the main heating stage. Some institutions dried or conditioned the samples before testing, while others tested the material without additional conditioning. In some datasets, the initial part of the measurement was not included in the submitted data, or the device did not record data until the initial stabilization criteria were met. Consequently, the apparent initial moisture content should be interpreted with caution when comparing results between laboratories.


One approach to reduce this variability would be to prescribe an identical drying and conditioning procedure for all participating laboratories. In practice, however, this would be difficult to implement consistently because of differences in available instrumentation and established measurement procedures. Alternatively, the temperature program could include an initial heating and drying stage followed by cooling and a second heating ramp, or an isothermal drying step before the main heating ramp, so that subsequent measurements start from a nominally dry sample. Such procedures may be appropriate when moisture itself is of interest or when moisture-sensitive quantities, such as heat capacity, are evaluated. For analysis focused primarily on the subsequent thermal decomposition, however, an additional experimental drying step is not necessary, since the data can instead be rescaled to the dry sample mass during post-processing as illustrated in Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA_N2_10K_drynorm}.

In the present analysis, the mass measured at 400~K, $m_{400}$, was selected as the reference dry mass. At this temperature, sample mass remained approximately constant after initial moisture loss and the main pyrolysis stage had not yet begun. Normalized mass is then calculated as $\frac{m}{m_{400}}$ (rather than as typically reported, $\frac{m}{m_{0}}$, where ${m_{0}}$ is initial sample mass); consequently, re-normalized mass at temperatures at $T<400$~K may exceed unity. Although this re-normalization greatly reduces differences between datasets prior to the onset of pyrolysis, it only partially improves the variability\footnote{\textcolor{red}{Consider measured residue yields calculated at both 700~K and 950~K from repeat measurements submitted by all institutions: at both temperatures, the standard deviation of $\frac{m}{m_{400}}$ is \# versus \# for $\frac{m}{m_{0}}$.}} in residue yield measured after the main decomposition stage (see Table~\ref{tab:wood_tga_results}). Across the investigated datasets, this correction increased the calculated residue yield by up to 2 percentage points at 700~K and 1.3 percentage points at 950~K. This demonstrates that differences in the measured residue yield cannot be explained by initial moisture content alone.

\textbf{Char Yield}\\ 
 Char (residue) yield is one of the key quantities derived from TGA measurements. Although it is often defined as a constant value (input parameter) in pyrolysis models, char is not a primary product~\cite{lewellen1977cellulose} and it is formed by competing mechanisms~\cite{broido1975char}, therefore residue yield is a function of decomposition conditions (e.g., heating rate, volatile residence time, gaseous environment, final temperature)~\cite{anca2016reaction, collard2014review,neves2011characterization}. In the present analysis, residue yields were therefore evaluated at both 700~K, shortly after the main decomposition stage, and 950~K, close to the end of the temperature program used by most participating laboratories. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:wood_tga_results}, residue yields at 700~K range from approximately 15~\% to 30~\% of the initial sample mass, whereas the corresponding values at 950~K range from approximately 7~\% to 20~\%. For most datasets, the residue yield decreases by approximately 5.5--7 percentage points between 700~K and 950~K. At these temperatures, hemicellulose and cellulose have largely decomposed, while continued mass loss can be associated with the much broader and slower decomposition of lignin, including secondary pyrolysis at elevated temperatures~\cite{yang2007characteristics}.
 
 Measurements from some laboratories demonstrated further mass loss of up to 10--16 percentage points at temperatures above 700~K (with an apparent second peak MLR around T~=~875~K). This suggests a potential secondary decomposition mechanism: oxidation of the residual char. The presence of a secondary reaction was further investigated by `Call', who performed additional tests specifically designed to ensure anaerobic conditions. In these tests, the purge-gas flow rate (nitrogen) was increased, the measurement chamber was carefully evacuated before each test, and the potential for backflow of oxygen into the system was eliminated. As seen in, Fig.\ref{Fig:TGA_pressure} this eliminated the secondary oxidation step. This confirms that the pronounced high-temperature mass loss observed in some datasets can result from oxygen entering or remaining in the nominally inert test environment. When such a secondary reaction is observed, the presence of oxygen should therefore be carefully considered. Its possible sources include insufficient evacuation of the system, short purge times before measurement, leaks or permeable tubing, low purge-gas flow rates allowing backflow, or contamination of the purge gas (source and/or transfer lines).

\textbf{Atmospheric Pressure}\\
The measurements by `Call' shown in Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA_pressure} provide an opportunity to examine the effect of pressure. In addition to the atmospheric-pressure measurements discussed above, this institution performed tests at 40~Pa under the same nominal heating rate of 10~K/min. The reduced-pressure measurements show a substantially lower residue yield immediately after the main decomposition stage compared with the 100~kPa measurements, suggesting that pressure may influence the amount of solid residue formed during pyrolysis. However, interpretation of the original pressure comparison is complicated by the residual-oxygen effects identified above. The additional 100~kPa measurements performed with improved oxygen control demonstrate that the pronounced high-temperature mass loss in the original atmospheric-pressure dataset was not caused by pressure itself. Consequently, while the results suggest an effect of pressure on residue formation, the available data from a single institution are insufficient to isolate or quantify this effect independently of other experimental conditions.

\textbf{Repeatability and Reproducibility}\\
Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA-peakMLR-N2_10K} summarises the peak normalized mass loss rate and the corresponding peak temperature obtained from TGA tests conducted in pure nitrogen at a nominal heating rate of $\beta$~=~10~K/min. This condition was selected for the main-text comparison because it contains the largest number of measurements and participating laboratories; corresponding results obtained at 5 and 20~K/min are provided in Appendix~\ref{App:TGA}. The intralaboratory repeatability of the peak temperature was typically within approximately 5~K, while the interlaboratory range was approximately 10~K when the identified outlier (`Cayuga') was excluded. For peak normalized mass loss rate, the intralaboratory variability was generally below $1\times10^{-4}$~s$^{-1}$, although values approaching $2\times10^{-4}$~s$^{-1}$ were observed for a small number of laboratories. The maximum relative intralaboratory variability was below approximately 15~\%. The interlaboratory range was approximately $4\times10^{-4}$~s$^{-1}$, corresponding to about 30~\%.

Compared with the MaCFP-2 measurements on PMMA~\cite{PreliminarySummary}, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:tga_macfp2_comparison}, the present MaCFP-4 results show slightly better interlaboratory reproducibility in peak temperature: the interlaboratory range decreased from approximately 15~K for MaCFP-2 to approximately 10~K for MaCFP-4, while the intralaboratory range remained approximately 5~K in both studies. In contrast, the relative variability of peak MLR is somewhat larger in MaCFP-4, both within individual laboratories and between laboratories, despite the absolute variability remaining of a similar magnitude. This difference reflects the substantially greater heterogeneity of the wood samples used in MaCFP-4 compared with the relatively homogeneous PMMA material investigated in MaCFP-2. \subsection{Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)}

A total of 87 DSC datasets were submitted by seven institutions. All DSC measurements were obtained as part of simultaneous thermal analysis (STA), with thermogravimetric and heat-flow data recorded during the same experiment.\footnote{Conventional DSC does not measure sample mass and is therefore best suited to quantitative evaluation of thermal events occurring without substantial mass change. When decomposition occurs, changes in sample mass can affect the DSC baseline and quantities normalised by sample mass. Simultaneous TGA-DSC measurements partly overcome this limitation by providing the corresponding mass-loss history, although STA generally represents a compromise compared with dedicated DSC measurements optimised specifically for calorimetric accuracy.} Of these measurements, 69 tests were conducted in pure nitrogen at nominal heating rates between 3~K/min and 50~K/min, with the largest number of 26 measurements obtained at 10~K/min. A further 18 tests were conducted at various heating rates in oxidative atmospheres containing 3~\%--21~\% oxygen in nitrogen.

Although most experiments were conducted using nominally constant heating rates, the actual sample heating rate, $dT/dt$, was not constant throughout every test. The largest deviations generally occurred during the initial part of the heating program, before the system approached the prescribed heating rate. As discussed for the TGA measurements and illustrated in Figure~\ref{Fig:TGA_dTdt_ranges}, the relevance of these deviations depends on the temperature range of the process being analyzed. Consequently, interpretation of DSC signals, particularly at lower temperatures, should consider the measured sample-temperature history rather than the nominal heating rate alone.

\textbf{Data Processing} \\
The key measurements recorded during DSC tests are heat flow to the sample, $\dot{q}/m_0$ (W/g), sample temperature, $T$~(K), and the time, $t$~(s), at which these values were recorded.  Both heat flow $\dot{q}$ and temperature $T$ were reported as time-resolved measurements. Prior to further analysis, the submitted heat-flow measurements were adjusted to a common sign convention such that endothermic events (e.g., heat flow to the sample during thermal decomposition, which peaks at approximately 600~K to 650~K) would be recorded as positive (increasing) events, with respect to the baseline. However, due to significant deviations in reported DSC data (potentially as a result of calibration, baseline correction, or instrumentation issues), negative and/or positive heat flow measurements do not necessarily indicate exothermic and/or endothermic events. Consequently, caution should be exercised when attempting to use these measurements for determination of `MaCFP-Wood' heat capacity, $c_p$, or heat of decomposition.\footnote{Note: `Ancona' DSC data were submitted with the comment, "DSC data serve as supplementary only and are not suitable for calibration." This dataset is thus included here for qualitative comparison and caution should be used if considering it for model calibration/property determination.} 

Most DSC tests were run at constant heating rates. Both heat flow to the sample and sample temperature were reported at regular time or temperature intervals. Much like TGA data, effectively this meant that heat flow to the sample was reported, in most experiments, approximately every 0.5~K. One institution reported data every 1~K, while another reported data at constant time intervals of 1~s. Because the reporting interval was not always defined directly in terms of temperature, and because measured sample temperature could occasionally decrease slightly from one time step to the next, the temperature spacing between consecutive reported data points was not strictly uniform in all datasets.

For all tests, heat flow signals were first processed using linear interpolation such that they were each reported at the same regular temperature intervals (i.e., every 0.5~K). Integral heat flow, $q/m_0$~(J/g), was calculated for each replicate test, $j$, using the trapezoidal rule:
\begin{equation}
\frac{q_{i,j}}{m_{0,j}} = \sum_{i'=1}^i \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\dot{q}_{i',j}}{m_{0,j}} + \frac{\dot{q}_{i'-1,j}}{m_{0,j}} \right) \left(t_{i'}-t_{i'-1} \right)
%we have to start from i'=1 otherwise at i'-1, we have a conflict
\end{equation}
Note: integral heat flows reported here are simply calculated from beginning to end of each test, thus care should be taken when comparing results submitted by different institutions that may have used different initial sample temperatures.

Due to substantial differences in heat flow measurements submitted by different laboratories under nominally identical heating and gaseous environment conditions, average DSC curves were primarily calculated separately for each institution. When repeated measurements were available from the same institution under the same experimental conditions, average heat flow and integral heat flow were calculated at each temperature point, $T_i$, using all available measurements within a 2~K interval centred at $T_i$:

\begin{equation}
\overline{\left(\frac{\dot{q}}{m_0}\right)_i}=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_{i,j}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}\sum_{i'=i-2}^{i+2}\frac{\dot{q}_{i',j}}{m_{0,j}}
\end{equation}

where $N_{i,j}$ is the number of available measurements from experiment $j$ within the interval $i-2 \leq i' \leq i+2$. This corresponds to a total temperature interval of 2~K around $T_i$. The standard error of the mean, $\sigma_{mean,i}$, was calculated at each temperature, $T_i$, as:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{mean,i}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}\sum_{i'=i-2}^{i+2}\left(\frac{\dot{q}_{i',j}}{m_{0,j}}-\overline{\left(\frac{\dot{q}}{m_0}\right)_{i'}}\right)^2}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_{i,j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_{i,j}-1\right)}}
\end{equation}

The same averaging procedure was applied to integral heat flow. Average curves are shown together with shaded regions representing $\pm 2\sigma_{mean,i}$.

The comparison in Fig.~\ref{Fig:DSC_N2_10K} also highlights several aspects that should be considered in future DSC measurements. Most of the submitted experiments were primarily designed as thermogravimetric measurements rather than optimized specifically for quantitative DSC analysis. Factors such as crucible material (conductivity), sensor configuration, and baseline correction can therefore contribute to the observed inter-laboratory differences~\cite{NetzschDSCBaseline,Salek2023thesis}. In particular, the submitted datasets do not always provide sufficient information to determine whether a separate baseline measurement was performed for the specific crucible and experimental conditions or whether an instrument-specific baseline correction was applied. Submission of the corresponding baseline measurements or correction procedure would therefore be beneficial in future MaCFP calls.

Sample moisture represents an additional source of uncertainty. The submitted samples were not conditioned to a common moisture content prior to testing, so measurements from different institutions effectively started from different initial material states. Evaporation of moisture produces a pronounced thermal effect at lower temperatures and can substantially disturb the heat-flow signal before the main decomposition stage~\cite{Salek2023thesis}. This complicates identification of the beginning of thermal decomposition and can influence the placement of the linear baseline between the onset and endset temperatures used for the heat-of-decomposition analysis described below. Moreover, heat flow is normalized by the initial sample mass, including moisture that is no longer present during the main decomposition stage; consequently, derived heats of decomposition are not strictly expressed on a dry-material basis. Different initial moisture contents can also directly contribute to differences in integral heat flow between laboratories.

The integral heat flow calculated over the entire experiment is particularly sensitive to these effects, as well as to differences in the initial heat-flow offset, baseline drift, and instrument-specific transients at the beginning of the heating program. Since even a relatively small offset can accumulate into a substantial difference in integral heat flow over a long experiment, these curves should be interpreted with caution. For quantitative determination of reaction enthalpies, integration of individual thermal events relative to an appropriate local baseline is therefore generally more meaningful than integration of the complete DSC signal.

\textbf{Heat of Decomposition} \\
Due to the deviations between DSC heat flow measurements submitted by different laboratories, the use of these data for direct determination of heat capacity or heat of decomposition requires caution. However, the simultaneous measurement of sample mass and heat flow in the STA experiments allows an estimate of the heat of decomposition of MaCFP-Wood to be obtained.

First, onset and endset temperatures of the primary mass loss reaction were defined (following the approach in Section~\ref{TGA_analysis}) as the lowest and highest temperatures, respectively, at which normalized mass loss rate exceeded 10~\% of the peak value. The onset temperature was subsequently adjusted, where necessary, to ensure that the linear heat-flow baseline between the onset and endset remained below the measured heat-flow curve up to the peak. On average, approximately 70~\% of initial sample mass was lost as samples were heated through this temperature range. Normalized sample mass loss due to decomposition across this temperature range was calculated:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta m}{m_0}=\frac{m_{\rm onset}}{m_0}-\frac{m_{\rm endset}}{m_0}
\end{equation}

Next, a linear baseline of heat flow to the sample crucible (neglecting the energy needed for decomposition) was defined between the onset and endset heat-flow values. The heat flow above this baseline was integrated over the corresponding time interval. Subtraction of this baseline from the measured heat flow during the period of active decomposition and scaling of the integrated value by measured normalized mass loss in this period produces an estimate of the total heat of decomposition of this reaction:

\begin{equation}
h_{\rm r}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{t_{\rm onset}}^{t_{\rm endset}}\left[\left(\frac{\dot{q}}{m_0}\right)-\left(\frac{\dot{q}}{m_0}\right)_{\rm baseline}\right]dt}{\Delta m/m_0}
\end{equation}
Individual values of $h_{\rm r}$ are presented in Table~\ref{Table_11} for each individual STA test conducted in a nitrogen environment.\footnote{The `Ancona' DSC data were submitted as supplementary information to the corresponding TGA measurements and were not acquired with the aim of maximizing DSC measurement accuracy. These values should therefore be interpreted with caution in quantitative comparisons.} Despite significant scatter in heat flow measurements from individual tests conducted by different labs (see Fig.~\ref{Fig:DSC_N2_10K}), the calculated values of $h_{\rm r}$ have a mean of 369~J/g and a standard deviation of 134~J/g.

\textbf{Specific Heat Capacity and TM-DSC} \\
Determination of specific heat capacity from DSC measurements requires more careful experimental control than a qualitative comparison of heat-flow curves. Accurate determination of $c_p$ depends on the instrument type and calibration, measurement procedure, sample mass and preparation, crucible characteristics, and reproducibility of the thermal contact between the sample, crucible, and sensor~\cite{mcnaughton2003differential,Salek2024}. For quantitative measurements, a three-run procedure is commonly employed, consisting of a blank measurement, measurement of a reference material with known specific heat capacity, and measurement of the sample under the same temperature program; synthetic sapphire is commonly used as the reference material~\cite{mcnaughton2003differential,Salek2024}.

A fundamental limitation is that the DSC signal can only be interpreted directly as specific heat capacity when no other significant endothermic or exothermic process occurs simultaneously. If moisture evaporation, thermal decomposition, curing, oxidation, or another thermal event occurs, the measured response represents an apparent heat capacity containing contributions from both sensible heating and the corresponding reaction or phase-change enthalpy. For this reason, dry samples and a temperature range below the onset of thermal decomposition are preferable for direct $c_p$ determination~\cite{Salek2024}.

Specific heating programs are therefore often used for $c_p$ determination. Besides conventional three-run procedures, temperature-increment methods~\cite{Salek2024} and modulated DSC approaches can be used to improve separation of heat-capacity-related effects from other thermal processes~\cite{mcnaughton2003differential}. In temperature-modulated DSC (TM-DSC), a periodic temperature modulation is superimposed on the underlying heating program, allowing the measured heat-flow signal to be separated into reversing and non-reversing components. The reversing component is primarily associated with heat-capacity-related behaviour, whereas non-reversing contributions include kinetically controlled thermal events.

TM-DSC measurements were conducted by one institution (`Mallard') using a custom temperature program. Samples were heated and cooled three times between 190~K and 430~K in a pure nitrogen environment, with an underlying heating rate of 3~K/min. Heat-flow measurements from the heating phases are shown in Figure~\ref{Fig:UMET_DSC_heatflow}. A pronounced feature is observed below approximately 360~K during the first heating cycle, particularly in the total and non-reversing heat-flow signals. This contribution is consistent with moisture loss from the sample and is strongly reduced in the subsequent heating cycles after the material has been dried during the first cycle.

\subsubsection{Heat Release Rate}

\textbf{Data Processing}\\
The key measurements recorded during MCC tests are heat release rate, $HRR$~(W/g); temperature, $T$~(K); and the time, $t$~(s), at which these two values are recorded. Both $HRR$ and $T$ were reported as time-resolved measurements. Tests were run, nominally, at constant heating rates of 30~K/min, 45~K/min or 60~K/min. Substantially more MCC data was recorded in this study on MaCFP-Wood as compared to the MaCFP-2 study on PMMA~\cite{PreliminarySummary}.

Heat release rate and temperature were reported either at regular temperature increments or at fixed time intervals. In most datasets, heat release rate was reported every 0.5 K; (one institution reported heat release rate at 2~Hz). Reporting frequency was not necessarily constant and, due to the nature of the experiment, measured sample temperature could actually decrease from one time step to the next. For all tests, heat release rate, $Q'$, and time, $t$, signals were thus first processed using linear interpolation such that they were each reported at the same regular intervals (i.e., every 0.5~K). 

Integral heat release rate at each time step $t_i$, $Q_i$, was calculated for each replicate test, $j$, using the trapezoidal rule:
\begin{equation}
	Q_i = \sum_{i'=1}^i \frac{1}{2} \left( {Q'}_{i',j} + {Q'}_{i'-1,j} \right) \left(t_{i'}-t_{i'-1} \right)
	%we have to start from i'=1 otherwise at i'-1, we have a conflict
\end{equation}
In this way, the time step, $\Delta t$, needed for this calculation could vary (see previous discussion on heating rates) but it would always correspond to the time needed for the sample temperature to increase by 0.5~K).

MCC data was analyzed to calculate average HRR and integral HRR as well as an estimate of measurement uncertainty for each of the measurement conditions. An effort was made to include as much data as possible in the generation of these average curves; however, clear outliers (full curves, not just individual data points) were not used for the calculation of mean and standard deviation values. These datasets are still plotted in Figs.~\ref{Fig:MCC_N2_30K} through \ref{Fig:MCC_O2-20_60K} with a note identifying them as outliers in the captions below their respective figures. For MCC measurements, datasets were defined as outliers, for example, when an negative integral heat release rate were observed (‘Crested’ tests in N$_2$ at 30~K/min) or peak heat release rate were greater than $50$~\% of the average of all other data.

When repeated measurements were available (submitted by the same or different lab(s) and conducted under the same incident heating conditions and gaseous environment) average heat release rate was calculated at each temperature, $T_i$, as the mean value reported from all (excluding noted outliers) repeated tests, $j$:
\begin{equation}
	\overline{Q'_i} = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_i} \sum_{j=1}^{N_j} \sum_{i'=i-n}^{i+n} Q'_{i',j}
\end{equation}
where $N_i$ is equal to the number of measurements from an individual experiment, $j$, reported in the interval $(i-n) \leq i' \leq (i+n)$ and $n=2$ because it is assumed that variations in repeated measurements should be minimal within a 2~K interval. $N_j$ is the number of replicate tests. The standard deviation of the mean, $\sigma_{mean,i}$, was calculated at each temperature, $T_i$, as:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{mean,i} = \sqrt[]{\left(\frac{1}{(\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_i)((\sum_{j=1}^{N_j}N_i)-1)} \sum_{j=1}^{N_j} \sum_{i'=i-n}^{i+n} \left( Q'_{i',j} - \overline{Q'_i} \right)^{2}\right)}
\end{equation}

For brevity, the main text of this document plots MCC measurement data only when multiple labs submitted repeated measurements under the same test conditions. Heat release rate and integral heat release rate curves (individual test data, along with average curves) from all MCC experiments conducted by all labs and under all experimental conditions (all heating rates and gaseous environments) are supplied in the Supplemental Information section.\\

After recording data with the MCC, a baseline correction must be performed to compensate for instrument drift. Submitted data was baseline corrected by the institutions performing the respective experiments. Previous studies have shown that differences in MCC results for the same material frequently arise from the application of different baseline-correction procedures~\cite{walters2012microscale}. Especially in tests on low heat release rate materials such as wood\footnote{The Total Heat Release of MaCFP-Wood is approximately 1/3 of that of polystyrene samples used for verification of MCC system performance~\cite{lyon2013principles}, while the peak HRR of MaCFP wood is only around 10$\%$ of that of polystyrene. This relatively low HRR means that signal noise and baseline correction issues can have a greater impact on MaCFP Wood HRR data.}, baseline correction can have a significant impact on reported HRR. This is even more significant for tests conducted at low heating rates (below the standard value of 60 K/min) or when using small initial sample masses. Some of the submitted data shows indications of potentially incorrect baseline corrections. In particular, for some tests, the integrated HRR decreases toward the end of the experiment (see Fig.~\ref{Fig:MCC_N2_30K}), despite its cumulative nature. In other cases, the integrated HRR is already above zero before the onset of any significant reaction, i.e. at the start of the experiment (see Fig.~\ref{Fig:MCC_N2_60K}). These features suggest that the baseline correction may introduce artificial heat release before the reaction or may not fully account for the signal after the reaction has ended. 

To assess the influence of the respective baseline corrections, participating institutions were asked to submit their raw data. Four institutions submitted raw data, which is available in the MaCFP repository under the label ``MCC-raw''. Figure~\ref{Fig:MCC_N2_60K_raw} shows the raw data as submitted. The Cayuga data stands out as a clear outlier. Following sample installation in the MCC, several minutes are typically required for the temperature and atmosphere in the pyrolysis chamber to stabilize. The Cayuga data suggest that the stabilization period may have been insufficient. When a test is initiated while the oxygen concentration remains elevated, the instrument interprets this initial oxygen level as the baseline corresponding to zero HRR. Consequently, when the oxygen concentration returns to approximately 20\% at the end of the test, the calculated HRR may remain artificially elevated (see Fig. ~\ref{Fig:MCC_N2_60K_raw}). The Cayuga data also exhibit smaller reactions preceding the main peak, which is also observed in tests performed with oxygen present in the pyrolysis chamber (see Fig.~\ref{Fig:MCC_O2-20_60K}.

\subsubsection{Tabulated values of interest}
Key features of MCC measurement data were identified as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Peak HRR and T peak}: The peak heat release rate and the temperature at which it was recorded. 
	\item \textbf{T onset}: A representative onset temperature of decomposition is calculated for MCC tests as the lowest temperature at which the integral HRR exceeds 5~\% of the total HR. This definition differs from that used to define onset temperature of decomposition based on mass loss in TGA tests to be consistent with the definition used when calculating a molecular level fire growth parameter~\cite{lyon2021molecular}.
	\item \textbf{Total HR}: Total heat released by combustion of the pyrolysis gases (a measure of the material's heat of combustion, when normalized by initial sample mass). Elevated Total HR values measured oxidation tests demonstrate the significant impact (on total heat release) of oxidation of residual char.
	\item \textbf{Fire growth capacity (FGC)}: A scaling parameter that accounts for intrinsic ignitability and burning rate of a material. Calculated as per Ref.~\cite{lyon2021molecular}, using Total HR and the T onset. 
	\item \textbf{Char yield}: Calculated as the final mass divided by the initial mass (reported here only when final mass values was reported in participant README files). 
\end{itemize}

The arithmetic mean and standard deviation (`Std Dev') of each of these values are reported in Tables~\ref{tab:MCC_values} for MCC experiments. Here, the reported uncertainty is one standard deviation of the average value of measurements made by one organization at one exposure level.

\section{Bench-Scale Tests}

\subsection{Cone Calorimeter}

Key measurement data from cone calorimeter tests include heat release rate normalized by the initial exposed sample area, $\dot{Q}''$~(kW/m$^2$), sample mass, $m$~(g), and in-depth or back-surface temperature, $T_{\rm back}$~(K). Each quantity was reported as a time-resolved measurement. In addition, initial sample mass, time to ignition, and time to flameout were reported for individual experiments where available. For most tests, recording frequencies were close to or equal to 1~Hz. For comparison and averaging, the submitted time-resolved data were linearly interpolated to regular 1~s intervals (1~Hz).

\textbf{Data Processing} \\
When repeated measurements were available (submitted by the same or different lab(s) under the same incident heating conditions), average HRR was calculated at each time step, $t_i$, as the mean value reported from all repeated tests:

\begin{equation}
  \overline{\dot{Q}i''} =
  \frac{1}{\sum{j=1}^{N} N_{i,j}}
  \sum_{j=1}^{N}
  \sum_{i'=i-n}^{i+n}
  \dot{Q}_{i',j}''
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of repeated experiments and $N_{i,j}$ is the number of available measurements from experiment $j$ within the interval $(i-n) \leq i' \leq (i+n)$. Here, $n=2$, corresponding to five consecutive measurements at 1~s intervals and therefore a 4~s time span. The standard error of the mean, $\sigma_{\mathrm{mean},i}$, was calculated at each time step, $t_i$, as:

\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\mathrm{mean},i} =
  \sqrt{
  \frac{
  \displaystyle
  \sum_{j=1}^{N}
  \sum_{i'=i-n}^{i+n}
  \left(
  \dot{Q}{i',j}'' -
  \overline{\dot{Q}{i'}''}
  \right)^2
  }{
  \left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N} N_{i,j}\right)
  \left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N} N_{i,j}-1\right)
  }}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Heat Release Rate}

Heat release rate measurements were available from 91 cone calorimeter experiments conducted by 10 institutions at incident heat fluxes of 25, 30, 45, 50, 60, and 75~kW/m$^2$, as summarised in Table~\ref{Table}. The largest datasets were obtained at 60, 30, and 50~kW/m$^2$, comprising 24, 20, and 17 experiments, respectively. Measurements were performed with the wood grain oriented both parallel and perpendicular to the exposed sample surface; however, the dataset is dominated by the parallel orientation, with 70 parallel and 21 perpendicular tests. Furthermore, perpendicular-orientation measurements were generally contributed by only a single institution at a given heat flux. Only at 75~kW/m$^2$ are perpendicular-orientation data available from two institutions.

Figures~\ref{Fig:Cone_30kWindivHRR}, \ref{Fig:Cone_50kWindivHRR}, and \ref{Fig:Cone_60kWindivHRR} plot measured area-normalized heat release rate (HRR) from individual experiments conducted at 30, 50, and 60~kW/m$^2$, respectively.  As seen here, HRR spikes after sample ignition before quickly dropping and maintaining a lower, relatively steady value (this behavior is typical of charring materials). Later in each test, a second rise in HRR is observed as the sample heats through to its back surface; typically, HRR does not return to 0 at the end of tests due to continued smoldering of the residual char. 

Measurements submitted by ‘Mallard’ (violet curves in Figs.~\ref{Fig:Cone_30kWindivHRR} and \ref{Fig:Cone_60kWindivHRR}) have a qualitatively different HRR profile - likely this is because this data comes from mass loss calorimetry experiments. In these tests, HRR is calculated based on temperature measurements made in the exhaust flow; this approach appears to underestimate HRR at the end of tests, though ignition and early heat release behavior appear to be well-captured. Apart from the Mallard measurements, the HRR curves generally retain a similar overall shape and show relatively good agreement in the timing of the first HRR peak, although its magnitude varies between experiments. Larger differences are observed for the second HRR peak, for which both the magnitude and timing can vary substantially between datasets, with shifts in peak position on the order of several hundred seconds in some cases. Due to variations in test duration between submissions (i.e., how long samples were allowed to continue smoldering later in each test), care should be taken when comparing quantities that depend on the total area under the HRR curve or on the state of the sample at the end of the measurement, including char/residue yield, total heat released, and heat of combustion.

\textbf{Effect of Grain Orientation} \\
To separate the effect of grain orientation from the overall experimental variability, the HRR data were averaged separately for samples with the grain oriented parallel and perpendicular to the exposed surface. The resulting curves in Figs.~\ref{Fig:Cone_Average_HRR_parallel} and \ref{Fig:Cone_Average_HRR_perpendicular} therefore no longer combine measurements with different grain orientations, and the remaining spread reflects repeatability within individual laboratories and reproducibility between laboratories. However, these sources of variability can still strongly affect the shape of the averaged curves. In particular, differences in the magnitude and timing of individual HRR peaks, as well as differences in test duration, can produce broadened or apparently additional peaks and abrupt changes that do not necessarily represent distinct physical burning phenomena.

\textbf{Critical Heat Flux} \\

Time-to-ignition measurements can also be used to estimate the critical heat flux for ignition, $\dot{q}_{\rm cr}''$. For thermally thick materials, assuming one-dimensional heat conduction and a constant ignition temperature, the following approximation for ignition at short times, when heat losses from the exposed surface can be neglected~\cite{Quintiere2006}:

\begin{equation}
t_{\rm ign} \approx \frac{\pi}{4}k\rho c
\left(
\frac{T_{\rm ign}-T_{\infty}}{\dot{q}_{\rm ext}''}
\right)^2
\end{equation}

where $k$, $\rho$, and $c$ are the thermal conductivity, density, and specific heat capacity, respectively, and $T_{\rm ign}$ and $T_{\infty}$ are the ignition and initial temperatures. This relationship suggests an approximately linear dependence of $t_{\rm ign}^{-1/2}$ on the incident heat flux. When surface heat losses are taken into account, this relationship can be approximated by:

\begin{equation}
t_{\rm ign}^{-1/2} \approx C\left(\dot{q}_{\rm ext}''-\dot{q}_{\rm cr}''\right)
\end{equation}

where $C$ is a proportionality constant. Consequently, the apparent critical heat flux can be estimated by extrapolating the linear relationship between $t_{\rm ign}^{-1/2}$ and $\dot{q}_{\rm ext}''$ to $t_{\rm ign}^{-1/2}=0$.

Figures~\ref{Fig:Cone_Ignition_time_inv_sqrt_mean_vs_Flux}(a) and (b) show this relationship for the calculated and reported ignition times, respectively, while the corresponding regression equations, coefficients of determination ($R^2$), and estimated critical heat fluxes are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:wood_cone_critical_heat_flux}. An approximately linear trend is observed for most datasets, although the regression slopes differ between laboratories. Differences are also apparent between grain orientations, with perpendicular samples generally exhibiting lower values of $t_{\rm ign}^{-1/2}$, consistent with their longer ignition times.

Despite relatively high coefficients of determination for most individual datasets, several regressions yield negative estimates of the critical heat flux, which are not physically meaningful. Using reported rather than calculated ignition times generally improves the estimates, but does not eliminate this problem. This demonstrates that the extrapolated critical heat flux is highly sensitive to relatively small differences in ignition time, even when the corresponding regressions exhibit good linearity.

One possible explanation is the insufficient resolution of ignition behaviour at low incident heat fluxes. As the lowest applied heat flux was 25~kW/m$^2$, the measurements may not adequately capture the behaviour close to the critical heat flux, making extrapolation to this region unreliable. This limitation is particularly relevant for wood, for which \cite{SpearpointQuintiere2001} reported changes in ignition behaviour at low heat fluxes and observed ignition at fluxes as low as approximately 10~kW/m$^2$.

Differences in sample preparation, particularly moisture content and conditioning, may also contribute to the observed interlaboratory variability. However, this information was missing or insufficiently documented for many participating institutions, preventing a systematic assessment of its influence. This highlights the importance of more consistent documentation of sample preparation and conditioning in future experimental datasets.

Furthermore, discrepancies between calculated and reported ignition times may partly arise from differences in time synchronisation or data acquisition resolution. For example, when originally submitted reported ignition times for `Rouen' were found to precede a noticeable increase in HRR by approximately 10--15~s in some experiments. HRR data was resubmitted after correcting for oxygen delay time, excluding this discrepancy. In addition, data from `Pekin' were recorded at 5~s intervals, limiting the temporal resolution of ignition time determination. These factors introduce additional uncertainty into the regressions. Consequently, the estimated critical heat flux values should be interpreted with considerable caution and should not be regarded as reliable quantitative measures of the ignition threshold.

\subsubsection{Heat of Combustion}

Heat of combustion, $\Delta H_{\rm c}$, was calculated based on cone calorimeter measurements as the total energy released per gram of mass lost (kJ/g). The total energy release was determined by integrating the measured HRR between the first and last times at which $\dot{Q}'' \ge 24$~kW/m$^2$, using the same threshold as for the calculated time to ignition. The resulting energy was divided by the corresponding mass loss over this interval. To assess the influence of the ignition time definition, an alternative calculation was also performed using the ignition time reported by the contributing institutions as the beginning of the integration interval, while keeping the end unchanged. The arithmetic mean and standard deviation (`Std Dev') of $\Delta H_{\rm c}$ are reported in Table~\ref{tab:wood_cone_results} for repeated measurements from all institutions that submitted cone calorimeter data. The reported variability corresponds to one standard deviation of the repeated measurements at a given exposure condition.

Figure~\ref{Fig:Cone_HOC_calculated_vs_reported} compares the heat of combustion calculated using the HRR-based ignition criterion with the values obtained using the ignition times reported by the contributing institutions. \subsection{Anaerobic Gasification}

Anaerobic gasification experiments were conducted by two institutions using two different experimental apparatus: a controlled atmosphere cone calorimeter~\cite{babrauskas1992cone} and CAPA~II~\cite{swann2017controlled}. Key measurement data from anaerobic gasification tests include: gasification mass flux, $\dot{m}''$ (g/(m$^2 \cdot$s)), and front and back surface temperatures, $T_{\rm front}$~(K) and $T_{\rm back}$~(K). Each quantity was reported as a time-resolved measurement.

Although plotted together in this section, measured mass loss rates and back surface temperatures may not be identical between different gasification tests, even at the same nominal incident heat flux due to differences in (a) initial sample thickness and (b) back surface boundary conditions. Specifically, CAPA-II sample were planed to just 8.7~mm thick, versus the ~25.4~mm thick samples used in controlled atmosphere cone calorimeter tests. Further, in CAPA~II tests, samples are not insulated at their back surface and, thus, this surface is subject to convective and radiative losses; this boundary condition is detailed elsewhere~\cite{swann2017controlled}.

\subsubsection{Gasification Mass Flux}

Mass flux in gasification experiments was calculated for each replicate test, $j$, at each time step, $t_i$, as the numerical derivative of measured sample mass using a time interval $\Delta t=5$~s as follows:
\begin{equation}
   \dot{m}_{i,j}'' = \frac{1}{A} \frac{\dot{m}_{i-2,j}-\dot{m}_{i+2,j}}{t_{i+2}-t_{i-2}}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the initial surface area of the sample. The subscript $j$ indicates a replicate measurement. Prior to further analysis, noise in individual mass loss curves (at 1~Hz) was reduced by applying a Savitzky-Golay filter (third order polynomial fit; 41 data point range). Due to the limited availability of measurement data, statistics are not calculated or presented for gasification tests.

\subsubsection{Thermal Diffusivity}

Thermal diffusivity was determined using both the Transient Plane Source (TPS) and Laser Flash Analysis (LFA) methods. \subsection{Reflectivity and Average Emissivity}

Spectral reflectivity measurements were submitted by three institutions for virgin `MaCFP Wood' and corresponding char samples. Figure~\ref{Fig:Reflectivity} shows the average measured reflectivity as a function of wavenumber. For virgin wood, measurements were available from Rouen, Canard, and Bali. Char reflectivity measurements were available from Rouen for samples produced at 20, 40, and 60~kW/m$^2$ in CAPA and from Bali for char produced in a cone calorimeter at 50~kW/m$^2$. Shaded regions represent $\pm 2$ standard errors of the mean of repeated measurements.

Based on the measured spectral reflectivity, an effective broadband emissivity was calculated by weighting the reflectivity with the Planck blackbody spectrum:

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(T) = 1 - \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\nu} \rho(\nu) B(\nu,T)}
{\displaystyle\sum_{\nu} B(\nu,T)}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\rho(\nu)$ is the measured spectral reflectivity and $B(\nu,T)$ is the Planck blackbody radiance:

\begin{equation}
B(\nu,T) = \frac{2\pi h c^2 \nu^3}
{\exp(hc\nu/k_B T)-1}
\end{equation}

Here, $T$ is absolute temperature, $h$ is Planck's constant ($h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J$\cdot$s), $c$ is the speed of light ($c = 3.000 \times 10^{8}$ m/s), and $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant ($k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K). Effective broadband emissivities were calculated between 293~K and 453~K for each averaged reflectivity spectrum and are reported in Table~\ref{tab:emissivity}.

\chapter{Summary}
\label{Summary}
\vspace{-1cm}
The measurement data and related analysis presented in this predecisional draft report should be considered as a preliminary summary of experimental work submitted to the 2026 MaCFP Condensed Phase Workshop. This summary is prepared for subject matter experts to provide critical review, for experimentalists to identify and correct minor errors in their submitted datasets (e.g., scaling or units issues; or mislabelling and/or accidental submission of incorrect data), and to provide modelers who wish to use these measurements for pyrolysis model development a suitable overview of the information available on the MaCFP Github Repository. 

For the purposes of model development, it may be desirable to have a `community average' of a particular dataset under given conditions (e.g., representative, average mass and mass loss rate curves from TGA tests or average heat release rate measurements from cone calorimeter tests). However, as presented in Section~\ref{exp_results} of this document, defining such an average is not necessarily straightforward. One of the valuable outcomes of this exercise is the demonstration that instrument calibration, sample prep, and test conditions and procedure may have a meaningful impact on measurement results. Thus, for some datasets, key features of the data are also reported (e.g., onset temperature of decomposition or time to peak HRR) along with the average of this specific data feature (mean of individual values measured by various institution). This reporting may limit the impact of apparent shifts (in time- or temperature-space) of time-resolved quantities. In this preliminary report, individual datasets are presented and average curves and/or key features of the data are calculated (excluding clear outliers); however, further analysis as to why exactly these differences exist -- i.e., are they simply stochastic or are they due to, for example, calibration differences or subtle variations in how exactly the tests were conducted -- is needed.

Ultimately, average curves presented here represent the aggregate of data as received, some of which may require corrections by the original submitting institution (e.g., if a dataset was incorrectly labeled or submitted). Valuable outcomes of the 2026 MaCFP Condensed Phase Workshop include the development of requirements for dataset quality and quantification of the inter-laboratory variability for comparable experimental datasets. If and when discrepancies arise in data that has been reproduced by different institutions under nominally the same test conditions, the intent is not to identify a `best' or `worst' dataset. Rather, we welcome all carefully conducted test data and properly formatted results that have been submitted by all participating institutions as these results help our community to understand: (1) test reproducibility and uncertainty, (2) instrument calibration, sample prep, and test conditions that may impact measurement results.

Deeper analysis of these results and recommendations for best practices will be addressed with feedback from the pyrolysis modeling community: both those who submitted experimental data and those who use these measurements to calibrate pyrolysis model parameter sets. This more detailed analysis will be included in the final version of this report. Additionally, a second, related, report will prepared afterwards with a focus on: (1) cataloging the current, state of the art approaches used to parameterize pyrolysis models, (2) defining the key pyrolysis model parameters of interest, and (3) assessing the impact of the variability of these parameters on predictions of material burning behavior and fire growth.

Modelers who wish to participate in the MaCFP-4 Workshop are asked to calibrate models (i.e., develop material property sets) that can accurately capture the decomposition behavior of these experimental measurements. Specifically, modelers are asked to provide: (1) material property sets,  (2) written and numerical descriptions of how these parameters were calibrated, (3) and numerical predictions of material thermal decomposition behavior in idealized 0D and 1D-heating scenarios (these numerical predictions are used solely for model-to-model comparison, $not$ model validation).  Further information on how to setup, format, and submit these modeling results are provided in the Guidelines for Participation Document~\cite{MaCFP4Guidelines}, which is \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/macfp-db/releases/tag/macfp-4.0}{available online}.
