\section{Introduction}

The Measurement and Computation of Fire Phenomena (MaCFP) Condensed Phase Subgroup is focused on enabling the fire research community to make significant progress towards establishing a common framework for the selection of experiments (and the methodologies used to analyze these experiments) when developing pyrolysis models. Early workshops -- MaCFP-2 (2021) and MaCFP-3 (2023) -- focused on a relatively simple, non-charring material: poly(methyl methacrylate), `MaCFP-PMMA' ~\cite{MaCFP2ExpSummary, MaCFP3Proceedings}. 
The measurement data (which can be used as targets for pyrolysis model calibration and validation) and calibrated material property sets presented at MaCFP-2 and MaCFP-3 have been uniformly formatted and well-documented. This standardized data naming and formatting, as well as inclusion of corresponding metadata (describing sample preparation, test setup, and experimental conditions) allows for efficient, automated analysis. All measurements (and related analysis tools) are maintained in a digital, version-controlled, and freely-available online repository: \url{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db}~\cite{MaCFP-cond-db}.

For MaCFP-4, the Condensed Phase Subgroup coordinated a new $anaerobic$ pyrolysis model calibration exercise focused on pine wood. This charring material was selected based on feedback from participants of the MaCFP-3 Workshop~\cite{MaCFP3Proceedings}, a majority of whom suggested (as a critical fire modeling need) studying a new material and/or studying charring/oxidation. This specific wood species was selected because of its widespread use in construction, furnishings, and standard fire testing (e.g., crib fires). MaCFP-4 pyrolysis modeling targets included $only$ anaerobic conditions; however, participants were encouraged to develop experimental datasets and corresponding models that will describe oxidation (as will be studied in detail at MaCFP-5). The intent is that this same wood will be studied in future MaCFP modeling exercises (e.g., oxidative pyrolysis, burning, and fire growth studies).

Limited quantities of `MaCFP Wood' were sent directly~\cite{MaCFP4Guidelines} to participants who committed to conducting experiments\footnote{All `MaCFP Wood' samples -- specifically, Eastern White Pine, $Pinus Strobus$ -- were obtained from the same forest/grove in Gardners, Pennsylvania (near Pine Grove Furnace State Park in the USA). Samples were felled, dried, prepared, and cut to size for milligram- and gram-scale experiments as described elsewhere~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp}}. No single approach for pyrolysis model parameterization was  suggested to participants especially because a key objective of MaCFP-4 was to catalogue current approaches used to parameterize complex pyrolysis models. Thus, participants were encouraged to follow their best practices to enable discussion of the similarities and differences of their approaches and results (i.e., final parameterization) without explicitly requiring a final validation versus a given test. Measurement data submitted by experimentalists participating in this exercise included: milligram-scale tests (e.g., thermogravimetric analysis, TGA, differential scanning calorimetery, DSC, and microscale combustion calorimetry, MCC), gram-scale testing (e.g., gasification and cone calorimetry experiments), and 'direct' property measurements (e.g., reflectivity or thermal diffusivity). A detailed summary of these experimental measurements is available elsewhere~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp}.

Modelers were not provided limitations or suggestions regarding their pyrolysis model parameterization (i.e., calibration) approach; however, they were required to use either (a) at least one of the milligram-scale datasets and one gram-scale experiment, or (b) at least two of the gram-scale experiments available in the \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/tree/master/Wood/Calibration_Data}{Calibration\_Data section} of the MaCFP repository. Modelers could supplement MaCFP data with any literature data that they deem necessary. Table~\ref{table:properties} lists all pyrolysis model parameters of interest (and their respective units) for this study.

\section{Model Development}
\label{sec:Models}
This section provides a brief description of the physical assumptions adopted by the different participating organizations when developing their respective models. Further information is provided in the Supplemental Information Section of this report (Sec.~\ref{sec:SI}).

\subsection{CORIA modeling assumptions}

The pyrolysis process was represented by three parallel decomposition reactions corresponding to the three main wood pseudo-components (nominally hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin, see eqn.~\ref{eq:CORIA-pyrolysis-reactions}). The initial mass fraction of these pseudo-components, are taken from literature \cite{marquez2021kinetic}: 0.163, 0.544 and 0.293, respectively. Dry wood was assumed in both the OD and 1D simulations. The kinetic parameters were identified from TGA experiments from the MaCFP-4 experimental database (\href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/tree/master/Wood/Calibration_Data/UMET}{UMET data set}), using the DAKOTA code and the NL2SOL optimization algorithm\footnote{Kinetic parameters for the CORIA reaction mechanism were optimized based on a fitness function that minimized the absolute error between calibrated and measured sample mass loss between 0 and 1500~s in 10~K/min TGA experiments.}, and the resulting kinetic mechanism was subsequently employed in the 0D and 1D simulations. Constant thermophysical properties, taken from the literature (Dinh PhD Dissertation~\cite{dinh2024developpement}), were considered throughout the simulations. For the 1D configuration, fibers were assumed to be oriented perpendicular to the exposed surface. The gasification simulations were performed with the PATO code \cite{lachaud2017generic}. Radiative heat exchange was prescribed at the exposed surface, with no convection. A zero-temperature-gradient condition was imposed at the rear surface.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:CORIA-pyrolysis-reactions}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Cellulose}      &\rightarrow n_1\,\mathrm{char} + (1-n_1)\,\mathrm{gas} \\
\mathrm{Hemicellulose}  &\rightarrow n_2\,\mathrm{char} + (1-n_2)\,\mathrm{gas} \\
\mathrm{Lignin}         &\rightarrow n_3\,\mathrm{char} + (1-n_3)\,\mathrm{gas}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{FSRI - UMD modeling assumptions}
The FSRI-UMD collaboration submitted two distinct models (i.e., two material property sets):
\begin{itemize}
	\item (\textbf{FSRI-UMD OPT}): an optimized property set, relying primarily on inverse analysis
	\item (\textbf{FSRI-UMD DM}): based as much as possible on directly measured properties	
\end{itemize}
Temperature-dependent thermophysical properties were considered throughout the simulations. \\

Both OPT and DM models employed the same thermal degradation kinetics and thermodynamics. The pyrolysis process was represented by six sequential first-order reactions, including one moisture evaporation reaction and five thermal degradation reactions\footnote{For the 1D Idealized Gasification simulations, the models were implemented in FDS version 6.10.1, neglecting the moisture evaporation reaction.}. Kinetic and energetic parameters were calibrated against STA (i.e., combined TGA and DSC) measurements obtained at multiple heating rates. The kinetic parameters were identified from STA experiments conducted by FSRI at 3~K~min$^{-1}$ and 30~K~min$^{-1}$ and by UMD at 10~K~min$^{-1}$ using a multi-stage hill-climbing optimization algorithm implemented in Julia and coupled with the \texttt{Pyrolysis.jl} model. 


Temperature-dependent specific heat capacities of the virgin material were determined through a linear interpolation of `directly measured' specific heat capacities (heat flow meter measurements between 25$^\circ$C and 45$^\circ$C) and apparent specific heat capacity (DSC measurements obtained between 150$^\circ$C and 185$^\circ$C; $\frac{dT}{dt}=10$~K/min). Char heat capacity was determined by fitting DSC data above the endset temperature of decomposition (STA experiments $\frac{dT}{dt}=10$~K/min).

In the FSRI-UMD OPT model, the heat capacities of intermediate species were fit as weighted averages of apparent specific heat capacities of virgin wood and char. Char and Ash were defined to share the same linear form. Moisture specific heat capacity taken from the NIST Chemistry Webbook~\cite{linstrom2001nist}. The heats of reaction were subsequently calibrated against DSC measurements acquired at 10 K min$^{-1}$. The FSRI-UMD OPT model also incorporated a state-dependent heat of sorption function that relies on temperature and moisture content. 

Density of the dried virgin component was determined from direct mass and volume measurements of regular shaped samples. The density of solid products of decomposition were determined from a combination of TGA and dilatometry data (both at 10K/min) for both models.
Surface emissivities were based on integrating-sphere measurements and depended on the incident heat flux. FTIR Integrating Sphere measurements were repeated on both virgin wood and char; emissitivities of intermediate species were assumed to be either (1) equal to one of these two values, or (2) equal to a weighted fraction of these two values. For both models, the absorption coefficient was assumed to be sufficiently high that all radiation was absorbed at the boundary. 

\subsection{NIST-StMU modeling assumptions}

The material property set submitted by NIST-StMU was calibrated using a hierarchical approach in which material properties are uniquely calibrated from individual measurements/datasets. For MaCFP-4, the NIST-StMU group provided a material property set based on the following measurements:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA): Decomposition reaction mechanism (\textcolor{red}{3**} parallel reactions), kinetics
	\item Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC): Heat capacities, Heats of reaction
	\item Microscale Combustion Calorimeter (MCC): Heats of combustion (flaming & charring) 
	\item  FTIR + Integrating Sphere: reflectivity
\end{itemize}

The decomposition process was represented by three parallel reactions corresponding to the decomposition of the main wood constituents (moisture loss is neglected). 
%%%\textcolor{red}{**find peaks first, use values of M and MLR at the peaks to infer kinetics; some iteration for total Mass to ensure conservation.}

Constant thermophysical properties were assumed throughout the simulations though unique specific heat capacities were prescribed for the virgin wood and the char. Property calibration was performed using Flammability data Automated Calibration Tools (FACT), which are a suite of automated Python scripts for estimating material properties from small scale tests.
 NIST-StMU properties were only used to predict 0D decomposition (i.e., idealized TGA/DSC tests) for the model-to-model comparison exercise.

\subsection{UCB modeling assumptions}
UCB submitted 12 distinct models (i.e., 12 material property sets). Each model used (1) one of three reaction schemes, (2) either constant or temperature-dependent material properties, and (3) thermophysical/transport properties calibrated to describe samples in which wood grains are oriented either parallel or perpendicular to the front surface of the wood. Each reaction scheme assumed that wood consisted of three main constituents -- cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin -- each of which can decompose in parallel. 

The three reaction schemes ranged from a simple three-reaction, parallel mechanism to more detailed, component-wise, multi-step reaction schemes. For each scheme, individual char species may be lumped together by giving them identical material properties. A brief description of these reaction schemes is provided below; more information is \href{https://github.com/MaCFP/matl-db/tree/master/Wood/Calibration_Results/UCB#readme}{available elsewhere}:.
\begin{itemize}
	\item Reaction scheme 1 (eqn.~\ref{eq:UCB1}): Single-stage, parallel reaction mechanism whereby individual components are each converted to char through one of three parallel reactions. Here, 6 total species are tracked, as each reaction produces a different char residue. 
	\item Reaction scheme 2 (eqn.~\ref{eq:UCB2}): multi-stage, parallel reaction mechanism whereby individual components are likewise converted to intermediate solid species, which are then converted to char residue through another set of parallel reactions. This gives a total of 6 reactions and 9 species
	\item Reaction scheme 3 (eqn.~\ref{eq:UCB3}): Unique reaction mechanisms for each individual component, based on TGA mass loss curves for each component measured in Ref.~\cite{hasburgh2020chemical}. The best reaction scheme for each component was found through trial and error: cellulose: 2-step serial, hemicellulose: 3-step serial, and lignin: 2-step parallel. These individual component reaction schemes are combined in parallel to form the overall reaction scheme for wood, consisting of 11 species and 8 reactions. 
\end{itemize}
 Kinetic parameters for each reaction scheme were identified from TGA measurements using the gpyro v0.8200 code coupled with the Shuffled Complex Evolution (SCE) optimization algorithm. The initial mass fractions of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin were first estimated from component-specific TGA data and subsequently used in the kinetic calibration. 

One-dimensional gasification simulations were performed using a modified OpenFOAM solver, porousFireDyMFoam, based on the gpyro biomass pyrolysis model. Thermophysical properties were calibrated against available gasification experiments. Both constant and temperature-dependent material properties were considered. Simulations were conducted for both parallel and perpendicular grain orientations. Property estimation for the gasification simulations was carried out using the Dakota optimization framework coupled to the one-dimensional model.

\section{Model-to-Model Comparison}

The objective of the MaCFP-4 condensed-phase benchmark exercise is to perform a model-to-model comparison. Since no common set of physical assumptions was imposed on the participants, and considering the diversity of the submitted modeling approaches, each contribution was treated as a separate model in the analysis. This approach makes it possible to assess the extent to which different modeling choices and assumptions affect the predicted results. Furthermore, the comparison of these contributions provides an opportunity to evaluate the individual influence of specific modeling assumptions on the simulation outcomes.

\subsection{1D Decomposition (Idealized TGA Experiments)}
Modeling participants were asked to perform simulations of idealized 0D experiments conducted at two constant heating rates (10~K/min and 100~K/min). As a limited number of modeling submissions included temperature-resolved heat flow rate predictions, only the TGA data (sample mass vs. time/temperature) is considered for further analysis. The TGA results are evaluated by comparing: (1) temperature-resolved mass loss rate (MLR) curves and (2) key features of this curve (i.e., peak MLR, the temperature at which this peak is recorded $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$, and onset temperature of decomposition, $T_{\rm{onset}}$. Here, $T_{\rm{onset}}$,  is defined as the lowest temperature at which normalized MLR exceeds 10~\% of its peak value (consistent with the definition used in the analysis of Experimental Results submitted to MaCFP-4~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp}). This analysis is conducted for two simulated heating rates, 10~K/min and 100~K/min.\\

To consistently compare predictions submitted by different institutions, calculated mass versus time/temperature were first interpolated onto a common grid (i.e., $\Delta T$~=~0.5~K). Next, these curves were smoothed by applying a Savitzky-Golay filter (third order polynomial fit; 41 data point range). Finally, a simple numerical derivative was used to obtain the corresponding MLR curves. This uniform processing enables the calculation of the mean (Eq. \ref{eq:mean_value}) and standard deviation (Eq. \ref{eq:std_deviation}) of all submitted datasets (values calculated at each temperature interval, $\Delta$, between 300~K and 1000~K. In these equations, $x_i$ denotes the predicted value at a given time or temperature, $n$ is the number of predictions, $\bar{x}$ (or $Mean$) represents the mean value, and $Std$ denotes the standard deviation.  Higher or lower standard deviations do not necessarily suggest more or less accurate predictions; this simply indicates the level of agreement between all of the model predictions submitted by participants.

\begin{equation} 
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i 
\label{eq:mean_value}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} 
Std = \sqrt{ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2} {n} } 
\label{eq:std_deviation}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:TGA_Predictions} plots the mass loss rate (MLR) curves predicted in idealized TGA tests conducted at heating rates of 10~K/min and 100~K/min. Table~\ref{tab:TGA_stats} summarizes key features of these curves -- $T_{\rm{onset}}$, $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$, and peak MLR -- and also shares the mean and standard deviation of experimentally measured values (for tests conducted at 10~K/min). Qualitatively, modeling results show strong agreement both between different predictions and as compared to experimental measurements. Since the present modeling exercise relies directly on the experimental data submitted to the MaCFP-4 Pyrolysis Model Calibration Exercise~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp}, part of the observed variability in the predictions can reasonably be attributed to the variability already present in the benchmark measurements themselves.

Considering only the results of 10~K/min tests, model predictions of $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$ match quite closely (within, on average, 7.7~K). For all three key features of data (Table~\ref{tab:TGA_stats}), based on their respective standard deviations, scatter in model predictions is approximately equal to that of experimentally observed variations. This is promising; however, it should be noted that each model was calibrated to a subset of TGA data that typically was closer to the mean of all datasets submitted. One interpretation of this result is that: (1) modelers may have correctly identified the most representative experimental data sets (effectively choosing not to analyze outliers and thus reducing scatter in their predictions) but (2) model calibration tools remain imperfect, though the significance of these deviations is not clearly understood by this comparison alone\footnote{The greatest deviation in $T_{\rm{onset}}$ predicted by different submissions is observed for the CORIA and NIST-StMU modeling results (lower and higher $T_{\rm{onset}}$, repetitively; both within the scatter of experimental results). The CORIA calibration approach optimized kinetic parameters ($A$ and $E$) but assumed initial mass fractions for each of the initial components (hemicellulose, cellulose and lignin) The NIST-STMU calibration scheme does not emphasize $T_{\rm{onset}}$, and instead focuses on accurately capturing peak MLR and $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$.}. 

The relative accuracy of model calibration approaches can be assessed by comparing these results to those of the MaCFP-2 pyrolysis model calibration excercise conducted in 2021 (on cast, black poly(methyl methacrylate), `MaCFP-PMMA'). In that exercise, predicted peak MLR was observed to vary by approximately $\pm$20~\% (versus 11.6~\% here) and $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$ varied $\pm$15~K (versus 7.7~K here). Further analysis is needed to quantify how variations in $T_{\rm{onset}}$, $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$, and peak MLR propagate when these models are applied to other scenarios or heating conditions (e.g., do these differences amplify when predicting ignition, burning rate, and flame spread / fire growth rate).

A first order assessment of the generalizability of these decomposition models was made by using the exact same mechanisms and associated kinetic parameters to predict mass loss in idealized TGA experiments at 100~K/min. As seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:TGA_Predictions}(b), models demonstrate equal or better scatter in $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$ but notable differences in peak MLR and the relative temperature range of secondary reactions, especially at high temperatures. This increase in scatter between predictions at higher heating rates is NOT observed~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp} in experimental data submitted at higher heating rates (though limited data is available above 20~K/min). Once again, compared to model calibration results submitted to the 2021 MaCFP-2 workshop, for idealized TGA tests conducted at 10~K/min, scatter in predicted peak MLR and $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$ has been reduced considerably: from $\pm$30~\% to $\pm$21~\% and $\pm$40~K to $\pm$6.7~K.

Previous modeling analysis~\cite{ding2023analysis} has suggested that shifts in $T_{\rm{pMLR}}$ (to lower or higher temperatures) have a much more significant impact on predicted flame spread and fire growth than similar shifts in peak MLR (higher or lower peak MLR at the same temperature). Thus, the close agreement in the predicted peak temperatures, suggests that the participating models capture the main features of the thermal degradation process in a relatively consistent manner. Overall, these results are encouraging, especially considering the complexity of the decomposition behavior of this wood and the relative scatter observed in the experimental data used for model calibration. 


\subsection{1D Decomposition (Idealized Gasification Experiments)}
Modelers were also asked to perform simulations of idealized, one-dimensional, radiation-driven gasification of `MaCFP-Wood'. Six scenarios were considered, including a combination of: two sample thicknesses (6~mm and 18~mm) and three external heat fluxes (10~kW\,m$^{-2}$, 30~kW\,m$^{-2}$, and 60~kW\,m$^{-2}$). This range of sample thickness and incident heating ensured that (1) at least some simulation conditions did not exactly match model calibration conditions and (2) enabled the comparison of material decomposition behavior under conditions that assess `burning behavior' (i.e., mass loss at higher incident heat fluxes) and heat flow through the material (i.e., back surface temperature rise at lower incident heat fluxes). The 1D gasification results are evaluated using the following two approaches: 
\begin{enumerate} 
\item Comparison of time-resolved predictions of mass loss rate (MLR) and back-surface temperature rise. 
\item Comparison of key characteristic quantities, namely the peak MLR, the time this was observed ($t_{\mathrm{pMLR}}$), the onset time ($t_{\mathrm{onset}}$), and the end-of-pyrolysis time ($t_{\mathrm{end}}$). \end{enumerate} 

As discussed above, the various contributions provided by the participating organizations are considered as independent model predictions because they are based on different modeling assumptions, parameter sets, and calibration strategies. The present comparison comprises one model from CORIA, two models from UMD-FSRI, and twelve models from UCB.\\

Qualitatively, the different models exhibit consistent mass loss behavior. At low heat fluxes, simulations generally display a single dominant MLR peak. When $q^{''}_{rad}=10$~kW/m$^2$, temperatures across the sample (see Figs.~\ref{fig:model-gasification-Tback}~and~\ref{fig:model-gasification-Ttop}) remain below 700~K, the upper range of thermal decomposition (Fig.~\ref{fig:TGA_Predictions}). At higher heat fluxes, predicted MLR reaches a first initial peak before decreasing and maintaining a relatively steady value; later in each simulation, a second peak in MLR is then observed just prior to sample `burnout', at which time back surface temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:model-gasification-Tback}) shows a second distinct increase (above the temperature range of decomposition observed in 0D simulations; see Fig.~\ref{fig:TGA_Predictions}). For each model, this period of quasi-steady mass loss (i.e., the duration between the two peaks in MLR) is considerably longer and more distinct as sample thickness increases. 

This mass loss behavior is common in cone calorimeter-type experiments performed on charring experiments~\cite{SFPEHandbookCone}: the formation of char at the sample's top surface can act as a thermal insulator (which can yield a period sustained, reduced MLR) but, after enough time has passed to allow the thermal wave to reach the back insulation layer, the sample is heated through its complete thickness and a second peak in MLR may be observed. Since oxidation processes are not considered in the present simulations, the appearance of two distinct peaks cannot be attributed to char oxidation. 
%Instead, it is likely related to the thermal decomposition of the different wood constituents and the development of temperature gradients within the sample. Hemicellulose typically decomposes at lower temperatures than cellulose, while lignin degrades over a much broader temperature range. As the heat flux increases, the heating rate becomes higher and these degradation stages may become more clearly separated in time, leading to the emergence of two distinct MLR peaks. In addition, the larger temperature gradients that develop within the material under higher thermal loads, together with the formation of a char layer at the surface, can further promote the separation of the dominant decomposition processes. 
As expected, the models also predict general trends in sample decomposition behavior as heating varies: as heat flux increases, peak mass loss rate (MLR) increases and total test duration decreases. Additionally, because samples cannot be considered thermally thick throughout the duration of heating, back surface boundary conditions affect measured burning rates and 6~mm thick samples support higher peak MLR than 18~mm thick samples.

Figures~\ref{fig:model-gasification-Tback} and \ref{fig:model-gasification-Ttop} present the back- and top-surface temperature histories predicted by the different models ($T_{back}$ and $T_{top}$, respectively). Similar to the mass loss rate (MLR) results, all models demonstrate the same qualitative behavior, though some distinct differences are observed between key features of these datasets. In general, $T_{top}$ quickly reaches a relatively steady value between 600~K and 800~K; this temperature increases with applied $q^{''}_{rad}$ (for sufficiently thick samples exposed to higher fluxes, $T_{top}$ can exceed 900~K). Predicted back-surface temperatures, $T_{back}$, achieve similar, quasi-steady temperatures between 600~K and 800~K, though this temperature rise is more gradual. Towards the end of each test, after sample `burnout', $T_{top}$ and $T_{back}$ all show a distinct second rise (to values between 650~K and 1000~K, increasing with applied ). These predictions suggest that relatively complete decomposition of wood samples should be observed at all incident heats fluxes and thickness studied in this work (although, residue yields decrease slightly as $q^{''}_{rad}$ increases).

To enable a more quantitative comparison of these results, key features of data -- onset time of decomposition, $t_{onset}$; peak MLR; time to peak MLR, $t_{peak}$; \textcolor{red}{residue yield, $\mu_{char}$}; and `end-of-pyrolysis time', $t_{end}$ -- are calculated for each simulation (all 15 models in 6 scenarios of varied sample thickness and $q^{''}_{rad}$). Here, $t_{onset}$ is defined as the first time at which MLR exceeds 1~g/s-m$^2$ (a critical mass loss rate for piloted ignition of solids~\cite{lyon2007criteria}, the same value used in the analysis of experimental results submitted to the MaCFP-4 Pyrolysis Model Calibration Exercise~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp}). A more conservative value, MLR$<0.1$~g/s-m$^2$, is used to define $t_{end}$. Mean and standard deviations of these key features of data are reported in Table~\ref{tab:FPA_stats_results} and, for each sample thickness; these values are plotted as a function of $q^{''}_{rad}$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:model-gasification-KeyFeatures}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:model-gasification-KeyFeatures}(a) highlights that predicted $t_{onset}$ (representative of sample ignition time) scales with incident heat flux as expected for thermally thick samples~\cite{quintiere2006fundamentals}: $\sqrt{\overbar{t_{onset}}} /propto q^{''}_{rad}$. However, at $q^{''}_{rad}=10$~kW/m$^2$, $t_{onset}$ is strongly affected by sample thickness (factor of 3x difference in average $t_{onset}$ for 6~mm versus 18~mm samples)\footnote{As reported in the analysis of MaCFP-2 modeling results~\cite{MaCFP2-ModelingSlides}, similar behavior is observed in predictions of MaCFP-PMMA gasification: ``sample thickness only impacts $t_{onset}$ at low heat fluxes."}. At this low heat flux, the standard deviation of $t_{onset}$ predicted by different models can vary by up to 2 minutes though, in relative terms, this represents only a 20~\% difference. At higher heat flux, for both sample thicknesses, relative scatter in model predictions of $t_{onset}$ vary much more substantially, on average between 50~\% and 60~\%. The relative standard deviation of $t_{end}$ also increases at higher $q^{''}_{rad}$, irrespective of sample thickness, though this dependence is not as pronounced. 

Sample thickness also strongly affects calculated mass loss: on average, comparing thinner samples versus thicker samples peak MLR is 1.9x greater when $q^{''}_{rad}=30$~kW/m$^2$ and and 1.4x greater when $q^{''}_{rad}=60$~kW/m$^2$. Scatter in peak MLR predicted by different models appears relatively independent of sample thickness and incident heat flux, with the calculated standard deviation of peak MLR varying between 15~\% to 20~\% for all cases. For most cases, scatter in $t_{peak}$ remains similarly low (10~\% to 19~\%); however, for the thicker samples, the relative standard deviation in $t_{peak}$ is approximately 60~\% when $q^{''}_{rad}=30$~kW/m$^2$ and 60~kW/m$^2$.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity Analysis of Individual Physical Assumptions} 
The UCB contribution provides a valuable dataset for assessing the influence of key modeling assumptions on gasification predictions. A key advantage of this simulation dataset is that it isolates the impact(s) of individual modeling assumptions while keeping many other model components unchanged (e.g., radiation modeling, and heat and mass transfer within porous media, choice of numerical solver, optimization targets, and calibration dataset). UCB investigated three classes of assumptions: 

\begin{enumerate} 
\item Thermophysical properties: temperature-dependent properties (\textit{TDEP}) versus constant properties (\textit{CONST}); 
\item Fiber orientation: parallel (\textit{PL}) versus perpendicular (\textit{PD}) orientation with respect to the exposed surface; 
\item Kinetic scheme: three different kinetic mechanisms were considered, as described in Section~\ref{sec:Models}. 
\end{enumerate} 

It is emphasized that parallel (\textit{PL}) versus perpendicular (\textit{PD}) material property sets are expected to predict different mass loss behavior, as they are calibrated to capture a true physical difference (i.e., radiant heating either along or against the wood grain orientation). In contrast, varying complexity in terms of the model parameters that describe decomposition kinetics or the temperature-dependence of material thermophysical properties should not necessarily yield different heating/decomposition behavior in a given set of conditions (though, presumably, models with greater complexity would hopefully be `more accurate').

The objective is therefore to understand the influence of each of these three modeling assumptions on: (1) qualitative shifts in MLR and Temperature rise (how do different sets of model predictions cluster together) and (2) quantitative differences in **\textcolor{red}{three} key features of the MLR datasets (between different models, how variable is the onset time of decomposition, $t_{\mathrm{onset}}$; peak mass loss rate, peak MLR; and the time at which peak MLR is observed, $t_{\mathrm{pMLR}}$). 
\textcolor{red}{**The parameter $t_{end}$ is not included in this comparison because an important part of the simulation were stopped before the pyrolysis ended.}\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\textbf{Qualitative observations (in order of strength of observation):}
\begin{enumerate}[]
	\item \underline{Impact of Wood Grain Orientation (perpendicular vs. parallel)}: 
	\begin{itemize} 
		\item For perpendicular cases (PD), back surface temperature initially increases more slowly than in parallel cases (PL), whereas the top surface temperature increases more quickly. This difference is more substantial for thicker samples and at higher incident heat flux, $q^{''}_{rad}$. This demonstrates the impact of reduced heat transfer across the wood grain versus along the wood grain. Correspondingly, MLR (peak and quasi-steady) and rate of rise of MLR are both substantially higher (factor of 2x) for PD versus PL cases. This is clearly apparent  when $q^{''}_{rad}=30$~kW/m$^2$ and 60~kW/m$^2$ but no clear trend is observed at $q^{''}_{rad}=10$~kW/m$^2$ (much lower overall MLR). 
	\end{itemize}
	
	\item \underline{Constant vs. Temperature-Dependent Properties}: 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The relative impact of prescribing TDEP or CONST thermophysical properties can be notable, but not always consistent for all cases. 
		\item For UCB-PD simulations, CONST and TDEP models generally cluster together and this behavior is cleaer (more distinct differences between groups) for thin samples versus for thick samples. For thick samples, UCB-PD-TDEP and UCB-PD-CONST predictions of MLR and $T_{back}$ all overlap; however, for thin samples, UCB-PD-TDEP and UCB-PD-CONST simulations form two clear/distinct groups.
		\item Typically, UCB-PD-CONST predictions of MLR and rate of rise $T_{back}$ are greater than UCB-PD-TDEP; however these differences are less clear (and can even reverse) for thicker samples, especially at high $q^{''}_{rad}$. 
		\item No clear trend is observed for UCB-PL cases: CONST and TDEP model predictions of MLR and $T_{back}$ all overlap.  
	\end{itemize}
	
	\item \underline{Reaction Mechanism}: 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For both parallel (PL) and perpendicular (PD) wood grain orientations, predictions of quasi-steady MLR seem less sensitive to variations in reaction mechanism than temperature-dependence of property values (see Figs.~\ref{fig:model-gasification-mlr} to ~\ref{fig:model-gasification-Ttop}: predictions group together more for a given color -- i.e., combination of TDEP/CONST and PD/PL -- than by line type [reaction mechanism]).
		\item For most of the UCB submissions, $within$ the same property set grouping -- i.e., [PD vs. PL] and [CONST vs TDEP] -- kinetics do not have the strongest impact on predicted mass loss and temperature rise. However, for UCB-PL-TDEP cases (which generally predict the lowest MLR) these variations can be significant. \footnote{Between the different UCB models, [PL-CONST-1] behaves as an outlier to this general statement, with notably higher predictions of MLR and surface temperature rise than other UCB-PL models (grain orientation parallel to radiant heat flux).} These results demonstrate that, although many studies in the literature highlight the sensitivity of predicted burning behavior to changes in kinetics or `reaction temperature' ~\cite{ding2023analysis,stoliarov2009effect, Linteris?},	differences in assumed reaction mechanism do not affect all models equally (nor is the impact the same at all fluxes and sample thickness considered here). One potential explanation for this behavior is that compensation during the calibration of other thermophysical properties can mitigate the impact of varied decomposition mechanisms (on predicted mass loss rate). Alternatively, this may suggest that the choice of calibration dataset (i.e., experimental data) is more important than assumed reaction mechanism and/or calibration targets within that dataset; this explanation $may$ explain part of why CORIA gasification predictions differed more significantly than various UCB models (see Fig.~\ref{fig:TGA_Predictions} and related discussion in Sec.~\ref{sec:Models}). %% SO - how exactly we calibrate our models may not be critical (depending on compensation w. other properties), but the dataset we use could still have an important effect.
	\end{itemize}

\end{enumerate}	
In general, the greater variation is observed between cases (especially during late-stage heating) when samples are thicker and exposed to higher fluxes (with results more sensitive to $q^{''}_{rad}$). At low fluxes, variations between models appears more random\footnote{Considering the rate of rise of back surface temperature at $q^{''}_{rad}=10$~kW/m$^2$, the relative order (which model predicts faster/slower increases temperature) of models submitted by different institutions changes significantly, depending on sample thickness (6~mm vs. 18~mm).}; However, at high fluxes, especially for thicker samples, models cluster based on property definition (i.e., combination of TDEP/CONST and PD/PL). This may suggest that various modeling approaches are more focused on reproducing global mass loss rate and improvement can be made by focusing on heat transfer through the material in response to lower $q^{''}_{rad}$. Given that $t_{onset}$ predicted by these models may vary by hundreds of seconds when $q^{''}_{rad}=10$~kW/m$^2$ (versus tens of seconds at $q^{''}_{rad}=60$~kW/m$^2$), these improvements may be significant.
	
It appears that calibration approach (e.g., data set used and/or model optimization targets) may have a stronger impact on model reproducibility than individual calibration decisions. For example, the two predictions made by UMD-FSRI (DM versus OPT) are not identical, but qualitatively they are more similar to one another than to the range of UCB model predictions. Further, the CORIA prediction is qualitatively the most different from all the others, especially in terms of $t_{onset}$ and rate of rise of MLR, $T_{back}$, and $T_{top}$ (this this model does predict roughly the same quasi-steady MLR).

For each test case, the influence of a given assumption was isolated by comparing pairs of simulations that differed only in the assumption under investigation, while all other modeling parameters were kept unchanged. The impact of the assumption was quantified using a symmetric relative error, and the corresponding mean value and standard deviation were subsequently computed. As an example, for the q=10 kW/m² and 6mm case, the effect of thermophysical properties was evaluated by comparing \textit{TDEP} and \textit{CONST} simulations while keeping both the kinetic mechanism and the fiber orientation identical. The same methodology was applied to assess the influence of fiber orientation and kinetic parameters across all heat-flux and thickness conditions considered in the benchmark.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity_flux_thickness} presents the mean relative errors and associated standard deviations obtained for the different modeling assumptions and operating conditions. The standard deviation of the relative error for $t_{onset}$, $pMLR$ and $t_{pMLR}$ is plotted as a function of the external heat flux for both sample thicknesses 6~mm and 18~mm in Figures~\ref{fig:FPA_CONST_vs_TDEP}, \ref{fig:FPA_PL_vs_PD}, and \ref{fig:FPA_kin_effect}. These figures highlight the influence of the thermophysical-property formulation (\textit{CONST} vs.\ \textit{TDEP}), fiber orientation (\textit{PL} vs.\ \textit{PD}), and the kinetic scheme on the predicted characteristics of the MLR curve, respectively. The observations from these figures and the accompanying table indicate that the influence of the different modeling assumptions depends strongly on the heat flux and material thickness being considered. Furthermore, the impact of these assumptions varies according to the quantity of interest under investigation. Another noteworthy observation is that the effects of the modeling assumptions are not monotonic. In other words, increasing the heat flux or the thickness does not necessarily lead to a consistent increase or decrease in the influence of a given assumption. This behavior highlights the complexity of the underlying physical processes and the strong interactions between the model physics and the characteristics of the studied scenario, namely the imposed heat flux and material thickness.\\

Accounting for the temperature dependence of the thermophysical properties affects the peak mass-loss rate differently depending on the sample thickness. For the thin sample, the influence of this assumption increases with increasing heat flux, whereas the opposite trend is observed for the thick sample. Overall, the impact of this assumption on $pMLR$ remains moderate, with variations below 20\%. Regarding $t_{pMLR}$, this assumption has almost no influence on the thin sample, whereas its effect can reach up to 80\% for the thick sample, which is substantial. The onset time, $t_{onset}$, is also affected by this assumption. Its influence is limited at low heat fluxes but can increase up to 27\% for the thick sample exposed to the highest heat flux.\\

It is interesting to note the strong similarity between the trends associated with the influence of fiber orientation and those associated with the temperature dependence of the thermophysical properties on the various characteristics of the MLR curve. It is important, however, to keep in mind that the mean values of the quantities of interest differ between the two cases, and that the corresponding absolute values of the standard deviations are also different. Nevertheless, the similarity in the observed trends, combined with the comparable ranges of standard deviation, suggests that both assumptions affect the model through a common underlying mechanism. A possible explanation is that assuming either a parallel or a perpendicular fiber orientation effectively corresponds to considering different thermophysical properties governing heat transfer within the solid. As a result, modifications induced by the fiber orientation assumption primarily alter the conductive heat transfer behavior, in a manner analogous to the temperature dependence of the thermophysical properties. Consequently, both assumptions influence the thermal response of the material through closely related physical mechanisms, which may explain the observed similarities in their effects on the MLR curve characteristics.\\

The influence of the kinetic mechanism on the MLR curve characteristics is generally moderate, although its impact varies significantly with both heat flux and sample thickness. For $pMLR$, the effect remains relatively limited, with standard deviations below 16\% in all cases. For the thin sample, the influence tends to increase with heat flux, rising from approximately 11\% at 10 kW/m$^2$ to more than 15\% at 60 kW/m$^2$. By contrast, for the thick sample, the influence decreases with increasing heat flux and becomes almost negligible at 60 kW/m$^2$. Regarding $t_{pMLR}$, the kinetic mechanism has a relatively small influence on the thin sample. However, its impact becomes much more pronounced for the thick sample. In particular, at 30 kW/m$^2$. This trend is remarkably similar to that observed for the temperature dependence of the thermophysical properties. The onset time, $t_{onset}$, appears to be one of the most sensitive quantities to the kinetic mechanism. For both thicknesses, the influence generally increases with heat flux. For the thin sample, the standard deviation increases from about 20\% to more than 25\% between 10 and 60 kW/m$^2$. A similar behavior is observed for the thick sample, for which the maximum influence reaches approximately 28\% at 30 kW/m$^2$. These results indicate that the kinetic mechanism plays an important role in controlling the initiation of thermal degradation. Overall, the kinetic mechanism primarily affects the temporal characteristics of the MLR curve rather than its magnitude. In particular, its strongest influence is observed on $t_{pMLR}$ and $t_{onset}$ for the thick sample, highlighting the important role played by the decomposition kinetics in controlling the progression of thermal degradation within the material.

Interestingly, the influence of the kinetic mechanism exhibits trends that are qualitatively similar to those observed for the temperature dependence of the thermophysical properties. In particular, both assumptions have a limited effect on $pMLR$ but can strongly affect $t_{pMLR}$ and $t_{onset}$ for thick samples. This similarity suggests that both assumptions modify the balance between heat transfer and decomposition processes, although through different physical pathways. While the temperature-dependent properties primarily alter heat penetration within the solid, the kinetic mechanism directly controls the rate at which the material decomposes once sufficient thermal energy has been accumulated.\\

\section{Summary}
In 2025/2026, the MaCFP Condensed Phase Working group organized a pyrolysis model calibration exercise to study the anaerobic decomposition of a charring material, Eastern White Pine (i.e., `MaCFP Wood'). **In total, \#\#\# experimental datasets from \#\# apparatus ** were submitted to this exercise by \# institutions submitted; a summary of these experimental measurements is provided elsewhere~\cite{MaCFP4PyrolysisExp}. This report highlights the contributions of \# institutions who submitted a total of 16 models that can be used to describe the 0D anaerobic thermal decomposition and/or 1D gasification of 'MaCFP Wood'. 

Models submitted to MaCFP-4 varied in complexity in terms of: reaction mechanism (e.g., `simple' 3-step parallel reactions versus multi-component mechanisms, each involving multiple that occur in series and in parallel), temperature-dependence of material properties (of virgin wood, char, and intermediate species), accounting for wood grain orientation, and selection of property calibration target(s) and/or approach. Careful comparison of the variability in modeling predictions and experimental measurements, both to each other and with respect to similar measurements and modeling results submitted to a previous MaCFP Pyrolysis Model Calibration Exercise conducted on cast black PMMA (MaCFP-2~\cite{MaCFP2ExpSummary} and MaCFP-3~\cite{MaCFP3Proceedings}) allow for a quantitative assessment of the state-of-the-art in pyrolysis model calibration. Specifically, this analysis helps to answer the questions:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item \textit{Experimental}: What level of uncertainty should we expect for each experimental dataset? (i.e., what’s the best we can do now) 
	\item \textit{Experimental}: What are the sources of this uncertainty, and how can this be improved?
	\item \textit{Modeling}: What does `good agreement' mean in modeling predictions (i.e., quantify model fit with respect to experimental uncertainty)
	\item \textit{Modeling}: What features of data are most critical (and under what conditions) when assessing model calibration accuracy (i.e., due to correlation between decomposition behavior at small scale [calibration] versus ‘large scale’ [validation: burning rate, flame spread, fire growth])?
	\item (How) have these uncertainties improved throughout the MaCFP effort?
\end{enumerate}
Items 1 and 2 above are discussed elsewhere. Item 4 has been lightly addres sed in the MaCFP-3 Proceedings~\cite{MaCFP3Proceedings} and will be a key focus of MaCFP-5. This report addresses items 3 and 5. The comparison of the different models highlights a good overall agreement in terms of qualitative predictions. All models reproduce similar trends for both TGA and 1D gasification simulations, which is encouraging. **Positive and negative aspects of the quantitative analysis presented in this work are highlighted as follows.

	\item Trends/what's good/bad. dependence on operating conditions
	\item Comparison to MaCFP-2 and MaCFP-3
	\item Suggestions on next steps/best practices, given the dependencies
\end{itemize}
\textbf{1D Heating and Mass Loss in Idealized Gasification Experiments}\\
\begin{itemize}[]

However, significant quantitative discrepancies remain. These differences can be attributed to variations in model assumptions, including the initial pine wood composition, fiber orientation, reaction mechanisms, thermophysical properties, and kinetic parameters. In particular, the use of different experimental datasets and calibration procedures for kinetic parameter identification contributes to the observed discrepancies. The results also indicate that the magnitude of these discrepancies depends on the operating conditions, such as the imposed heat flux, sample thickness, and the quantity of interest. Under certain conditions, the predictions converge and provide close estimates, whereas under others, phenomena such as radiative heat transfer, porous medium effects, or thermally thin/thick behavior become more influential. Overall, the study suggests that the observed differences are primarily driven by modeling assumptions and input data rather than by the numerical framework itself. Future work should focus on validation in order to complete the analysis.
